# Ostraka: The Twelve Magistars, Powers & Magic System
### Compiled from the notes of Clary, First Archivist of the Order of the Clockwork

*Part of the Ostraka canon reference set. Companion volume to Cosmology & Origin.*

---

### A Note Before the Roster Begins

I have written elsewhere about how the Dominion rose and how the dragons were made, and I will not repeat that account here beyond what's necessary to make sense of what follows. What I want to do in this volume is something I think most records of the Twelve have failed to do properly: treat them as who they were, not only as what they became. Every dragon sleeping beneath every Grove on this world was, once, a person with a name their mother gave them, a title they were proud of, and a specific, particular way they went wrong. I find that people remember the monster far more easily than they remember the man, and I have come to believe this is a mistake — not a sentimental one, but a practical one. You cannot predict a pattern you refuse to look at closely. I have looked closely. This is what I found.

## I. The Roster, and Why It Took So Long to Settle

I will say plainly that establishing this roster was not simple, and I do not think any honest account should pretend it was. Across the fragments I've recovered — old campaign ledgers, half-burned temple records, the private correspondence of people who thought they were writing about someone else entirely — the same twelve dragons keep reappearing under names that don't match, tangled up with three or four names that turn out to belong to no one at all. I spent the better part of a year chasing a dragon called Vrak before I understood that Vrak had never existed — the name was a placeholder some long-dead scribe had used before the true names were settled, copied forward by later hands who had no way of knowing they were copying a blank. Kael, Zylos, Valkor, Ghal, Kyras, Igni: all of them the same kind of ghost, names attached to real dragons by writers who no longer remembered, or never knew, which dragon they meant. I have retired every one of them here, and I want to be transparent about that rather than simply presenting a clean roster as though it had always been clean.

What remains, once the ghosts are cleared away, is twelve. Not thirteen, not fifteen — twelve mortal rulers of the Dominion, one seated over each of the Twelve Powers, one bound beneath each of the twelve Sacred Groves. I set them down here as I have finally come to understand them, elemental sigil and all, because the metal a wielder carries and the god a dragon once was are, I have come to believe, the same question asked two different ways.

And one deliberate exception, kept apart from the twelve on purpose, because she was never one of them in the first place: **Sh'lar, the 13th Shadow**, a Fae advisor who chose her corruption rather than having it forced upon her, and who has never once, in any record I've found, been bound to a Grove. I have written about her at length in the companion volume. Here it is enough to say she does not belong on this list, and the fact that people keep trying to fit her onto it says more about how frightening she is than any description I could offer.

I want to correct one thing about Ratio specifically before I go any further, because I have seen it done badly in other people's summaries and it has always bothered me. Ratio is not simply "logic," the way a merchant balances a ledger or a soldier calculates a trajectory. Ratio is the Power that imposes *pattern* on things — logic and calculation are what a mind does once it recognizes the pattern already there, but the domain beneath them is broader: repetition, structure, predictability, the underlying shape that makes an otherwise chaotic system legible to anyone willing to look for it. This is, I think, the only honest way to explain why Metrion's patronage sits so comfortably over the Meridian Chain — a cartel whose entire business is finding and enforcing pattern across a chaotic ocean, not performing raw arithmetic for its own sake.

## II. Who They Were Before

I have come to think of what follows as the most important section of this entire volume, and I suspect it will also be the least read, because it is easier to fear a dragon than to grieve the person it used to be. I record it anyway.

Every one of the Twelve was a mortal ruler of the Dominion before "the Shattering" — I use the word the way most surviving accounts do, though I've written elsewhere about how much more specific and how much more deliberate that transformation actually was — remade them into something else entirely. Each carried a mortal name, a mortal title earned through some particular skill or cruelty, and each fell in a way that mirrors, with an almost unbearable precision, what they eventually became.

**Aurgenas** was born Leoric, called the Serpent for the surgical precision of his political poisons — he built his power not through armies but through infiltration, delighting in the elegance of his own betrayals, believing that controlling information made a man something close to a god. He was not wrong about the power of information. He was catastrophically wrong about what he'd become once he had enough of it. Today he is the Gilded Solvent, and everything that made Leoric dangerous — the liquidity, the dissolving of whatever stood in his way — simply never stopped.

**Carulkem** was Vahn, the Shield, a protector before he was anything else, and I confess this is the fall I find hardest to sit with, because so many of the others chose their corruption in some recognizable way and Vahn, as best I can tell, did not. He is the Furnace Eternal now.

**Gavusrix** was Sorel, the Alchemist, a master of blood and biology whose gifts were meant, at least at first, to heal. She is the Quicksilver Mother today, and I want to note — because I got this wrong myself for longer than I'd like to admit — that "the Fecund Plague" is not her true title. It survives only as regional slang among the Sump-Kin and the Toad Barons, who have their own reasons for preferring a crueler name than the one she actually carries.

**Lophex** was Eldra, the Keeper, a guardian of knowledge before he became the thing that unmakes it. He is the Geometric Void now, and I have come, in my own research, to believe his is one of the more genuinely tragic falls on this list — a keeper of secrets who became, in the end, a secret no one can safely learn.

**Metrion** was Kyros, the Architect, and I find something almost too neat in his fall to be comfortable with: a builder of cities became, in the end, the Crystalline Mind — logic taken so far past the point of sanity that it stops resembling logic at all and starts resembling something closer to a beautiful, endless, unlivable geometry.

**Opecten** was Rictus, the All-Seeing, and remains, in his way, exactly what his title promised, only turned monstrous. He is the Mirror Lord now.

**Stagus** was Isolde, the Bulwark, endurance given a name before it was given a curse. She is the Unyielding Glazier today.

**Termhill** was Lyra, the Oracle — and I want to be careful here, because I have seen her name misattributed more than any other dragon on this list, borrowed by writers describing threats that were never hers. She is the Black Star now, and her true domain, Omen, is confirmed everywhere I have found it recorded reliably.

**Tiraton** was Grom, the Living Siege, weight and inevitability given a mortal face before they were given a Grove. He is the World-Weight today.

**Tyrustis** was Zeila, the Unbroken, and I find a particular grim irony in that name given what she became: the Mad King, willpower turned so absolute it consumes the will of everyone around it.

**Vecelo** was Anya, the Razor, speed and precision before they became something closer to silence itself. He is the Silent Echo now.

**Virantor** was Malaki, the Golden Voice, and of everyone on this list I think his fall is the easiest to understand and the hardest to forgive: a voice that once earned trust honestly became, in the end, the Burning Soul — charm weaponized so completely it no longer needs the truth to work.

Each of these falls reads, I have come to believe, as a magnified and twisted echo of whatever made that person remarkable in life. The Architect who built cities now unmakes the shape of things entirely. The Alchemist who healed now cultivates monstrous, uncontrolled abundance. Sh'lar remains the exception in every sense — not a ruler who fell, but an advisor who chose the fall willingly, with her eyes fully open, which I have always found somehow worse.

## III. What Each Power Actually Feels Like

I have spent a great deal of my career interviewing wielders — carefully, at a respectful distance, and always with someone present who could pull me back if the interview went somewhere it shouldn't — and I want to record here what they've told me, because most accounts of the Twelve Powers describe them only in terms of what they *do*, and almost never in terms of what they feel like from the inside, or what a bystander would notice standing nearby.

A wielder of **Mass** carries a crushing, deliberate gravity with them wherever they go — a subsonic grinding just beneath the range of easy hearing, an oppressive pressure in the air that makes a room feel smaller than it is, small objects rolling almost imperceptibly toward them as though the wielder had quietly become the center of a very small world.

**Nexus** announces itself before it does anything else: a furnace-blast heat, the smell of scorched iron, veins and eyes that glow faintly when the wielder's temper rises past whatever line they'd meant to hold.

**Ordo** is the opposite in every sense — sudden silence, sudden cold, breath crystallizing in air that was warm a moment before, a wielder who becomes icy and unnervingly, unnaturally still, the way a held breath is still.

**Lex** carries weight of a different kind: a crushing pressure like the air before a thunderclap that never quite arrives, a voice that resonates in layers as though more than one throat were speaking at once, a posture too perfect to be entirely comfortable to look at.

**Motus** is loud in every sense a body can be loud — a deafening sonic crack on activation, a wielder who cannot stop moving, tapping, vibrating, speaking faster than a listener can comfortably follow.

**Flux** smells before it does anything else: sulfur, acid, the shimmer of heat-warp in air that shouldn't be hot, a wielder whose movements go loose and boneless in a way that reads, to anyone watching, as faintly wrong.

**Vita** is rampant, uncontrolled growth given expression — I will note here, because I have seen the confusion elsewhere, that this describes the Power itself, not a competing title for the woman who carries it now.

**Ratio**, true to what I've already said of it, manifests as reality snapping back into balance whether it wants to or not — chain-lightning, a kind of reality-glitch that looks, to an untrained eye, almost like the world stuttering. It smells of burnt ozone, crackles faintly with static, and its wielders are, without exception in every account I've gathered, twitchy and permanently frizz-haired, as though the Power itself refuses to let its carrier sit still.

**Lux** is blinding truth given light — a ringing silence, shadows erased entirely by sudden brilliance, skin gone translucent at the edges. Its true masters, I am told by more than one reliable witness, cast no shadow at all, which is a detail I still find unsettling every time I write it down.

**Omen** is decay accelerated and time gone slightly out of sync with itself — the smell of dust and old tombs, a sudden and unearned sorrow in anyone standing nearby, a shadow that lags a half-step behind its owner's movement, clocks that stop without explanation in a wielder's presence.

**Aura** is emotion weaponized — the scent of a memory that isn't the bystander's own, hearts that race or drop without cause, a wielder with dilated, captivating eyes that make it difficult to look away even when every instinct says to.

**Anumis** unmakes the structural truth of whatever it touches — a heat-mirage ripple in the air, a sickening hum just under hearing, a wielder whose eyes dart as though reading invisible text, fingers permanently stained with ink they can't quite explain.

I record all of this not merely as color, though I will admit I find it fascinating in its own right, but because I believe it matters practically. A trained eye can identify which Power someone is channeling from across a crowded room, well before that person does anything overt enough to be called a threat. This has saved lives. I intend for it to go on saving them.

## IV. What Magic Costs

I want to be direct about something the more romantic accounts tend to skip past: magic is not free, and pretending otherwise has gotten people killed in front of me. Overuse produces what my order calls **Aetheric Fever** — a racing heart, veins that darken visibly beneath the skin, and, if the wielder ignores their own exhaustion long enough, a violent backfire that can leave lasting scarring, a fractured mind, or outright burnout. I have watched this happen exactly once, close enough to do nothing but watch, and I do not intend to describe it further here. It is enough to say the cost is real, and anyone who tells you otherwise has either never paid it or is lying to you about the price.

## V. The Great Wheel

This is, I think, the piece of my research I am proudest of, and also the piece that took the longest to get right — long enough that I once wrote down the wrong answer and believed it for the better part of a year before a fresher source corrected me.

The twelve metals bound to the Twelve Powers do not simply sit beside one another in some arbitrary list. They form a single, closed, unbroken loop — twelve steps that lead back to where they began, each element's Power destroyed by the one that comes before it on the wheel, and immune entirely to the one that follows. I want to walk through why this ordering, and not the other one I once believed, and I think the reasoning matters as much as the conclusion. Fire burns arcane secrets — the oldest metaphor there is, forbidden knowledge given to the flame. Decay conquers life, because it always eventually does. Poison corrupts spirit before it ever touches the body. Ice melts under light. Once you see the pattern this way, the older, more mechanical "absorbs-and-releases" ordering I once recorded looks exactly like what it was: technically consistent, but missing the reason a person could actually use it in a fight.

| Element | Power / Dragon | Destroyed By (Weakness) | Immune To (Shield) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lead | Motus (Vecelo) | Aura (Phosphorus) — Spirit/Charm | Lux (Chromium) — Radiant/Light |
| Chromium | Lux (Opecten) | Motus (Lead) — Sonic/Velocity | Ordo (Titanium) — Cold/Stasis |
| Titanium | Ordo (Stagus) | Lux (Chromium) — Radiant/Light | Lex (Lithium) — Psychic/Willpower |
| Lithium | Lex (Tyrustis) | Ordo (Titanium) — Cold/Stasis | Flux (Gold) — Acid/Reaction |
| Gold | Flux (Aurgenas) | Lex (Lithium) — Psychic/Willpower | Ratio (Silicon) — Shock/Electricity |
| Silicon | Ratio (Metrion) | Flux (Gold) — Acid/Liquidity | Nexus (Tungsten) — Fire/Heat |
| Tungsten | Nexus (Carulkem) | Ratio (Silicon) — Shock/Logic | Anumis (Bismuth) — Arcane/Secrets |
| Bismuth | Anumis (Lophex) | Nexus (Tungsten) — Fire/Heat | Mass (Osmium) — Force/Gravity |
| Osmium | Mass (Tiraton) | Anumis (Bismuth) — Arcane/Secrets | Omen (Iridium) — Necrotic/Time |
| Iridium | Omen (Termhill) | Mass (Osmium) — Force/Gravity | Vita (Silver) — Poison/Mutation |
| Silver | Vita (Gavusrix) | Omen (Iridium) — Necrotic/Decay | Aura (Phosphorus) — Spirit/Charm |
| Phosphorus | Aura (Virantor) | Vita (Silver) — Poison/Mutation | Motus (Lead) — Sonic/Velocity |

Any conduit built on this wheel carries a real risk if it fails — what my order calls a **Blowout**: a localized gravity flip, instant biological petrification, or living flesh remade without warning into inanimate metal. I have seen the aftermath of two. I do not intend to see a third.

There is a darker layer to all of this that I want to record plainly, because I believe understanding it is the only real defense against it. A wielder actively channeling one of the Twelve Powers becomes, in that moment, a living node of this same wheel. They gain total immunity to whatever Power sits ahead of them in the cycle — and a catastrophic, wholly disproportionate vulnerability to whatever sits behind them. This is not an abstract observation. It is the entire operating principle behind the Mandate of the Chain, an anti-magic order composed entirely of Nulls — people biologically immune to the psychic feedback of the weave — who capture exhausted wielders and execute them using chains forged from their own precise Breaker Metal. It is, in the most literal sense available to me, a wielder's own power turned back against them, and I have never found a way to describe it that makes it sound less brutal than it is.

## VI. The Worm Cults, and Why They Do What They Do

I want to close this volume with the material I consider the most operationally important I have ever compiled, because I believe too many people — including, for a time, myself — misunderstand what the Worm Cults are actually trying to accomplish, and misunderstanding a threat like this is how it eventually succeeds.

A Sacred Grove cannot be broken from outside by force. I want to state that clearly, because it is the single fact that makes everything else about the cults make sense. The only way to break a seal is to starve it — to cut off the chaos flow feeding it long enough that the pressure inside finally exceeds the strength of the containment holding it. This is why cultists do not attack the prisons directly, whatever the more sensational accounts might imply. They attune themselves, as completely and as dangerously as their bodies allow, to their patron's specific flavor of chaos, and they gather upstream in numbers — living sponges, willingly suffering horrific mutation in exchange for siphoning the flow away before it ever reaches the Grove. They are starving the prison by drinking the poison meant for it themselves, and I have come to believe that this is, in its own terrible way, an act of genuine devotion, however monstrous the object of that devotion happens to be.

I record the full roster here, cult by cult, because I believe every name on this list deserves to be known by the people whose safety depends on recognizing it. I want to note something I did not understand for a long time myself: a cult's target is not always the same as its patron dragon's home nation. A cult attacks whoever is most vulnerable to its particular flavor of chaos, which is very often not the nation that happens to host the Grove itself.

I want to explain that last entry, because I think it is the strangest and most important item on this whole list. The Mandate does not know it belongs here. They believe themselves the sworn enemy of everything the other thirteen cults represent — and mechanically, in the most literal sense, their zealotry accomplishes exactly what the others are trying to accomplish, simply by accident. Their chains recharge the very seals they believe themselves to be threatening. I have written elsewhere about why this happened, and I will not repeat the full account here, except to say that I consider it one of the more genuinely humbling ironies I have ever documented, and I do not use the word *humbling* often.

I want to end this volume with the thing that keeps me awake more reliably than any of the rest of it. The true danger was never a single dragon slipping free — though that alone would be catastrophic beyond anything I can adequately describe. The true danger is what happens *next*. A freed Magistar becomes, instantly, a walking vacuum for primordial chaos, absorbing the ambient flow at a rate no calculation I've run can call anything less than exponential, forcibly diverting the leyline streams meant for the other eleven prisons in the process. Those prisons would starve in turn. The rest would follow in a cascade with no natural stopping point, and the shattered continents — no longer held together by the Lattice that currently keeps them whole — would drift apart into the very void this entire system was built to hold closed. This is why my order treats even a single successful cult, however small, however contained it appears, as an extinction-level problem rather than a regional one. I would rather be accused of overreacting to every isolated cell we find than be right, even once, about which one we let slip past us.
