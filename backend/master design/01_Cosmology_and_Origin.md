# Ostraka: Cosmology & Origin
### Compiled from the notes of Clary, First Archivist of the Order of the Clockwork

*Part of the Ostraka canon reference set.*

---

### A Note Before the Record Begins

I have been accused, more than once, of writing history the way other people write ledgers — too many columns, too little feeling. I make no apology for it. Feeling is what a person has left when they've run out of facts, and I intend for this account to be the kind that never runs out. Where the record is certain, I will say so plainly. Where it is not, I will say that too, rather than smoothing the gap over with something that merely sounds finished. The world was nearly unmade once by people who preferred a satisfying story to an accurate one. I will not add to that number.

I should say, too, that I did not come to this work from idle curiosity. I lost a village to a thing with no name and no mercy, on a morning I remember more clearly than I remember most of the years since. Logic did not bring my father back. It has, however, kept me from losing anything else I could not afford to lose, and it is the only shield I have ever found that actually holds. This is why I write the way I write. Take it or leave it.

Now. To the beginning — the true beginning, not the one most tellings rush past to get to the parts with dragons in them.

---

## I. What Ostraka Is Not

Before the true record, the false one, because you will hear it regardless of what I write here, and you should know exactly what you're rejecting.

Most people are raised on some version of the old cosmology: that the world is a shattered eggshell, its continents drifting fragments held apart by nothing but strained tension, floating in a void that would swallow every one of them the instant that tension slipped. Sailors along the southern routes still tell each other about the "Hydrological Nightmare" — the idea that the oceans themselves sit one crack away from draining into some molten deep below. It is a vivid image. I understand its appeal. I have stood on a ship's deck during a bad Meander snap and felt, for one unreasonable moment, that the story might be true.

It is not true. I have spent the better part of two decades and the resources of an order most of the world doesn't know exists confirming this, and I will state it here as plainly as I know how: Ostraka is a normal, solid globe. It has never floated. No continent has ever torn loose from its neighbor, because there was never a neighbor to tear from — the ground beneath every nation on this world has stood exactly where it stands since long before the first city was raised on it. What moves, what has always moved, what will go on moving until someone finally finishes the work my order exists to do, is not the world itself. It is the energy passing through and across it. The ground holds still. The danger travels. Once you understand this distinction, a great deal that seemed inexplicable about our world resolves into something merely difficult, and difficult, I have found, can always eventually be solved.

To understand why the world behaves as it does — why the seasons run the length they run, why Dragonstone falls from the sky only once in a great while, why twelve dragons sleep beneath twelve mountains and must, under every circumstance, remain there — you cannot start with any nation's founding, or even with the first mortal footprint on this soil. You have to go back to before there was soil to speak of at all.

---

## II. The Divine Age

I want to take this slowly, because most accounts do not, and I think the rushing has cost people an understanding they deserved to have.

In the very beginning — and I mean this without embellishment, before there was an Ostraka, before there was a sun to warm one, before there was anything at all that a mortal mind could stand inside and call *a place* — there were gods. Not one god, not a pantheon arranged around a single throne, but a scattering of them, ancient and separate and largely indifferent to one another, existing across a darkness so total that the word "universe" barely does it justice. I have read three separate fragmentary accounts of this era, and each agrees on almost nothing except this single point: for a very long time, nothing happened that a story could be told about. There was simply divinity, patient and unhurried, existing.

What ended that patience — or perhaps simply what the patience eventually produced, the way stillness given enough time produces motion regardless — was propagation. The gods reproduced. I do not know the mechanism, and I distrust every account that claims to, because every one of them describes it differently and none of them agree on so much as whether the process took an instant or an age. What matters, for our purposes, is the result: where there had been darkness, there began to be light, scattered across the void in countless small points, each one a god's continuing presence rather than a god's absence. This is, as far as any surviving record can tell us, the origin of the stars themselves — not decoration scattered across a sky for mortal eyes to admire later, but the literal, ongoing bodies of divine beings, spreading outward the way any living thing spreads when given room and time enough to do it.

I linger on this because I think it matters that the stars are not backdrop. They are population. Whatever comes later in this account — the sun that watches us, the moon that was once an eye, the small dead planet that drifts too close every so often and reminds an entire world what grief looks like from orbit — none of it is scenery. It is all, without exception, someone.

---

## III. The Birth of the Fae

Among the uncountable gods scattered across that first spreading light, one settled near a young, unremarkable star and noticed something the others, in their vastness, might easily have overlooked: a small and improbable people, newly arisen nearby, entirely unprepared for how briefly and how easily a young world can lose its warmth.

I have not been able to recover this god's name. I suspect it was lost deliberately, in the same act that made a name unnecessary — because what he did next was not the kind of thing a name survives. He resolved that his people, the ones who would come to be called the Fae, should never know a world without a sun to warm them. And so he did not merely promise to protect that sun, or watch over it from some safer distance the way a patron watches a favored temple. He *became* it. He folded his own form into the star itself, binding himself to it so completely and so permanently that the star and the god ceased to be separate things at all — undying now, unable to fail his people even if some later grief made him wish he could.

He did not manage this without cost, and the cost is written across our sky to this day, for anyone who knows to look. His eye, still turned toward the world he'd given himself to protect, became a moon. In time it would be called **Erranith** — I will have a great deal more to say about Erranith before this account is finished, and none of it will be comfortable. And the remainder of his old form — the parts of him that could not follow the rest into the sun, the discarded husk of a body no longer needed — was left behind entirely, drifting, hollow, and inert. It would one day be called **Cruorbus**, "the Blood Orphan," though at the time of its leaving it was orbiting nothing at all. It simply drifted, the way a wound drifts before anyone notices it's there.

---

## IV. The Age of Harmony

For an age after that sacrifice — and I use the word *age* deliberately, because nothing in the surviving record suggests this was some brief golden interlude before the trouble started; every account agrees it was long, genuinely long, longer than any single nation's entire history since — the Fae lived exactly as the sacrifice had promised. They grew beneath an eternal, undying sun. They grew close to one another the way any people do when given centuries of uninterrupted peace. And, crucially for what comes later, they grew close to other gods as well — not strangers meeting for the first time when trouble eventually arrived, but old friends, already fond of this small, luminous people, already inclined to care what became of them.

I mention this deliberately, because it will matter enormously later, when a different tier of divinity enters this account seemingly out of nowhere. They are not out of nowhere. They were always watching. They simply had no reason, yet, to intervene.

---

## V. Man Appears

Into this long and settled peace came something the Fae had not built and could not have anticipated: mortal life, rising the way mortal life so often does, without ceremony and without asking permission. The old records call them, collectively, "the twelve types of man" — a phrase I have never been fully satisfied with, since it implies a unity these peoples plainly did not have, but it is the phrase that survives, and I record it as I find it rather than improving on history to suit my own preferences.

The Fae — who were, by any measure available to either side, older, wiser, and immeasurably more powerful — did what elder peoples so often do when confronted with a younger one hungry to learn. They became mentors. I want to be fair to them here, because most accounts of this period rush toward the betrayal that follows and skip the years, plural, in which this mentorship actually worked. The Fae taught. Mankind learned. For a genuine span of time, the arrangement was exactly what it appeared to be.

It did not hold. I do not know whether the failure was inevitable, built into the shape of the arrangement from the start, or whether it was simply misfortune, the kind of slow curdling that happens to almost any relationship between unequal partners given enough time. What I know is that the mortal races the Fae had raised up began to want what their teachers possessed and they did not — magic, permanence, a freedom the Fae had never intended to share past a certain point. Guidance curdled into hunger. Whatever patience had existed on either side wore through.

---

## VI. The Fae Seal Themselves Away

Rather than allow what had been offered freely to be taken by force, the Fae made a choice that would, though none of them could have known it, define the next several thousand years of this world's history far more than anything they'd done before. They turned their own magic inward and sealed *themselves* away — not as exile, not as punishment of anyone, but as withdrawal, pure and deliberate. Twelve vaults, hidden across twelve idealized corners of the world, each built to keep magic in and mankind out.

I want to be precise about something here, because I have seen the confusion repeated in three separate texts and I do not want to repeat it a fourth time. Those twelve vaults are the same twelve sites we call, today, the **Sacred Groves** — the very locations that would one day become the prisons of twelve chaos dragons. The Groves were never built as prisons. Not originally. They began as sanctuaries, raised by a frightened, retreating people to keep themselves safe from those they had once called students. It was only much later, after everything I am about to describe, that the same walls which had once kept magic *in* were pressed into service keeping something monstrous *in* as well. Nothing about their essential nature changed in that repurposing. Only what they were asked to hold.

---

## VII. Man Goes to War

Denied what the Fae would no longer share, mankind did what mankind reliably does when denied something it wants badly enough: it turned on itself instead. Twelve warlords rose from among the twelve types of man, each carving dominance over their own people, each moving with what the record describes as grim inevitability toward a final confrontation that would have destroyed all twelve at once, along with everyone unfortunate enough to serve under them.

That battle never happened. On the eve of what should have been mutual annihilation, the twelve warlords did something I still find remarkable every time I reread the account, precisely because it is so rare: they stopped. They looked at what they were about to lose. And they struck a deal instead of a blow. If their strength was joined rather than spent against each other, that strength could be turned toward a far richer target — the Fae, sealed behind their own walls, sitting on more power than any single warlord could have dreamed of claiming alone.

---

## VIII. The Dominion

What followed is remembered — by those honest enough to remember it accurately — as **the Dominion**: the joined rule of the twelve warlords across **thirteen generations**, an empire built not on the conquest of foreign lands but on the patient, deliberate cultivation of blood magic. Sacrifice, practiced and refined. Body-hosting, the transfer of a ruler's essence into a fresh vessel when the old one failed him. Spirit stasis, holding a mind suspended against the slow erosion of mortal time. Through methods like these, the Dominion's rulers extended their own lives far past anything "the twelve types of man" should ever have been capable of, generation folding into generation under the same twelve hands — or the same twelve *claims* to hands, at least; I have found no reliable record of how many times any single ruler's original body was actually replaced.

In that long reign, they did something considerably worse than simply rule. They harnessed the whole planet's substance to their ambitions, and in doing so they warped the twelve types of man — their own subjects, their own people — into monstrous, twisted forms. I have not been able to find a single surviving account that lingers on this the way I believe it deserves. Most texts move past it in a sentence, eager to reach the dragons. I will not do the same. Whatever walked this world by the end of the Dominion's thirteen generations, it was not entirely the people who had begun it, and I think that fact should trouble us more than it typically does.

---

## IX. The Barriers Fall

Even thirteen generations of patient, monstrous cultivation could not, on their own, break twelve Fae vaults built to withstand precisely this kind of siege. But nothing holds forever against sufficient pressure applied over sufficient time, and eventually, inevitably, the barriers weakened. Facing what must have looked, from behind those failing walls, like certain defeat, the Fae made their own desperate concession: rather than be conquered outright and stripped of everything at once, they offered to help the Dominion freely, on their own terms, in exchange for not simply being taken.

I have turned this decision over many times in my own thinking, and I have never been able to condemn it as cleanly as I once expected to. It was very likely the worst decision available to them. It was also, given the alternative actually in front of them, perhaps the only one that made any sense at all in the moment it was made. I record this not to excuse what came next, but because I think an honest history owes its subjects that much context, even when — especially when — the subjects in question are about to do something unforgivable.

---

## X. Sh'lar Is Turned

Among the Fae who came forward to broker this new and desperate alliance was one whose name has since become, across half the cultures of this world, a byword for betrayal itself: **Sh'lar**. Whatever terms she was sent to secure, whatever caution she carried into that negotiation, she was seduced instead by the sheer speed and violence of what the Dominion's blood magic could accomplish — a kind of power her own patient, careful people had never permitted themselves to touch. She turned. She taught the Dominion's rulers how to reverse-engineer the immortality the Fae had always kept for themselves alone, and worse — far worse — she revealed to them something even the Fae had never fully reckoned with in all their long centuries of study: a **thirteenth, hidden force** beneath the Twelve Powers that gave the Fae's entire cosmology its underlying shape. The Dominion called it the School of Primordial Chaos. I have come to believe it deserved a more frightening name than that, but the one it was given is the one that survives, and I will not pretend otherwise for the sake of drama.

Emboldened by what Sh'lar had taught them, the Dominion moved from careful negotiation to open extraction. Fae sacrifices — real ones, forced ones, not the willing exchange that had first been offered — were attempted.

---

## XI. The Exodus

The Fae who remained understood, at last and far too late, exactly what they had allowed themselves to become: not partners, not equals, but fuel. Rather than wait to be consumed one by one in whatever order the Dominion found convenient, they performed what history has come to call **the Exodus** — a single, desperate working that tore open a passage and sent every surviving Fae fleeing through it, away from Ostraka entirely, toward a place that would come to be known as **the Barrens**.

I want to correct something here that I myself believed for longer than I am comfortable admitting. The Barrens is not some separate dimension, reached through some impossible fold in reality that no living mind could ever properly describe. The Barrens is **Erranith** — the same moon that was once a god's watching eye, now the last, cold refuge of a people with nowhere else left to run. Every account I have ever recovered describing "where the Fae went," whether it names a Barrens or names an Asylum on a dead world, was describing precisely the same place. They only ever disagreed on what to call it, and I confess it took me embarrassingly long to notice they weren't actually disagreeing about anything at all.

The working that carried them there did not close cleanly behind them. It tore something else in the leaving: a wound in the fabric of this world, in the exact place where the ritual was performed. That wound has a name, and I do not use it lightly. It is called **the Weeping Wound**, and to this day, it has never fully closed.

---

## XII. The Divine War

A tear of that magnitude does not go unnoticed by beings who measure their attention in centuries rather than years. The Exodus drew the eye of a tier of divinity that had, until that exact moment, kept itself entirely apart from Ostraka's affairs — **the Elder Gods**, old friends of the Fae from the long, settled Age of Harmony I described earlier, arriving now to a world their friends had already been driven from.

What followed should, by any reasonable measure, have been simple: the Elder Gods against the Dominion's ascendant warlords, a contest the gods ought to have won without particular difficulty. It was not simple. At first — very likely through Sh'lar's continuing influence, though I have never recovered a full account of the exact mechanism — the Elder Gods were tricked into turning on one another, spending their strength on a war among themselves while the true threat gathered its power entirely unmolested.

It was mortals, in the end, who broke that trick, not gods. **The traitor captains** — soldiers serving within the Dominion's own armies, not warlords themselves, nothing close to divine — recognized the deception for exactly what it was and exposed it, redirecting the Elder Gods' fury at last toward the enemy that actually deserved it. I have given each of these twelve their own full portrait in the companion volume on Notable Figures, and I will not repeat the whole of it here — but I want to say plainly, before moving on, that they matter more than almost anyone else in this entire history, and I do not say that lightly.

---

## XIII. The Last Stand

By the time the Elder Gods understood what they were truly fighting, the Dominion's twelve warlords were already dangerously close to finishing what Sh'lar had set in motion generations earlier. At the final confrontation, she bought them the time they needed the only way she had left to give it: she faked a parley, offering the appearance of negotiation and peace, while behind that mask the twelve warlords completed their ascension ritual in secret.

It worked. **The dragons were born**, and in the same breath they began destroying everything within reach. Whatever the twelve warlords had imagined ascension would feel like, whatever control they believed they would retain over the power they had spent thirteen generations chasing, none of it survived contact with what they actually became. They did not become gods, whatever they had told themselves in the years of preparation. They became something closer to hurricanes that happened to remember their own names.

---

## XIV. The Second Sacrifice

The Elder Gods still standing after the Divine War's worst fighting understood, watching the newborn dragons rampage across a world already half-broken beneath them, that they would lose everything outright unless they acted immediately and completely. So they gave everything they had left to give. They dissolved their own divine forms into the twelve largest surviving pieces of the shattered Celestial Clockwork, and in that dissolution released the raw energy that would become the **Ley-line Lattice** — the containment system, the leyline network, the entire circulatory apparatus that still, to this day, holds this broken world together.

Sh'lar, true to everything she had already shown herself to be, poisoned even this. She tainted the sealing ritual with her own life and essence, ensuring that whatever containment resulted would carry a permanent, deliberate flaw — a leak built into the system's very foundation, not an accident of imperfect craftsmanship. Every Blowout since. Every successful Worm Cult. Every crack in every seal any of us has ever documented. All of it traces back to that single, spiteful act.

---

## XV. The Engineer, Alone

One Elder God remained standing after the others gave themselves to the working: the youngest among them, remembered now only by the title he earned in his final, exhausting days — **the Engineer**. It fell to him, alone, to finish what his fellow gods had begun and could no longer stay to complete. He took each of the twelve newborn dragons and sealed them, one at a time, inside massive shards cut from Cruorbus's own drifting remains — meaning the Dragon Prisons themselves are, quite literally, built from pieces of a dead god's discarded body, a fact I find far too few people appreciate given how often they walk within sight of one. This is distinct, I want to stress, from **Dragonstone** itself, which is not prison-material at all but meteoric debris from Erranith. I have seen the two confused in writing meant to be authoritative, and I correct it here as plainly as I know how: they are not the same substance, and treating them as such has led at least one expedition I am aware of into a very costly error.

The Engineer moved each finished prison to its Sacred Grove — the same twelve vaults the Fae had built for themselves so long before, still structurally sound, now asked to hold something considerably worse than they were ever built for. He wove the leylines that carry excess chaos away from each prison toward a single convergent point: **the Convergence**, sitting exactly where the Weeping Wound was first torn open. Chaos flows to the Convergence, and from there flows onward, out through the very crack the Exodus left behind — a crack the Shattering then widened further still, when the dragons were born and the world broke beneath them. I want to be unambiguous about this, because I have seen it described as metaphor in texts that should have known better: that crack is not a metaphor. It is a literal channel connecting Ostraka to the void beyond, and it has never fully sealed.

The labor took decades. By the time it was finished, the Engineer's own strength was spent entirely, and he laid himself down where he stood. Where he lay, he petrified, becoming over time the central mountain range that anchors this world's geography to this day. His skull and torso became what we now call the Sacred Spire. One arm became the Snowspire Plateau; the other, the Sheer Cliffs. His legs became the Flower Valley. Every nation that lives in his shadow, whether its people know it or not, lives quite literally atop the body of the god who saved them.

---

## XVI. The Gift

Before he faded past the point of return, the Engineer gave one final gift to the twelve traitor captains who had helped him carry this labor through to its end — the same soldiers who had once exposed the Elder Gods' deception during the Divine War. He granted them immortality: the very prize the Dominion's warlords had murdered, betrayed, and sacrificed their way toward across thirteen generations, given here instead, freely, as payment for loyalty rather than extracted through blood.

These twelve — **the First Wardens** — were, by every account I have been able to verify, the last surviving humans on Ostraka. Whatever remained of humanity beyond them did not survive what followed. The Divine War, the ascension's cataclysm, and the dragons' first rampage had already come close to finishing the work on their own; what remained was finished by the world itself in the years after. Even with the prisons built and the worst of the violence stabilized, the surviving human pockets faced a planet that had not yet settled, chaos still flaring in every direction, none of the resilience the animal-folk would later carry into that same danger. Worse — and I confess this is the detail I find hardest to sit with — thirteen generations of Dominion blood magic had left every remaining human genetically altered and hypersensitive to magic, in a world now saturated with exactly the kind of raw, untamed chaos their bodies were least equipped to survive. They did not stand a chance. The twelve captains truly were the last of their kind, and I do not believe they were spared by accident.

---

## XVII. How the Animal-Folk Came to Be

The Wardens' long vigil did not begin over an empty world. Bathed continuously in what has come to be called **the Divine Echo** — the resonance still bleeding faintly from the Engineer's petrified body, saturated further by the leaking energy of the Twelve Powers all around them — the ordinary wildlife of the twelve primordial biomes underwent an accelerated leap toward true sentience. Every animal-folk people alive on Ostraka today, every faction I have catalogued in the companion volume to this one, is the evolved inheritor of that radiation. None of them arose separately. They rose from the same source, in the same twelve places, shaped afterward by whichever dragon's particular flavor of chaos happened to color their corner of the world.

To ensure the Sacred Spire would never lack for guardians as the old generation inevitably faded, one of the Wardens — remembered only by the title the Prophet — wove a network of hidden traps into the landscape, triggered whenever a newly sentient, Spark-bearing creature crossed one. The first activations were not gentle, and I do not believe they were meant to be. Waking suddenly to the full, terrifying truth of what actually holds this world together is not a thing any mind survives with its composure intact, and the shock of that mass awakening is precisely what later ages came to call the Era of the Widening Eye. It is not a poetic label. It is a literal description of what happened to an entire generation of newly sentient minds, and I have chosen to record it that way rather than let the poetry obscure the fact underneath it.

The Prophet also laid down what survives in fragments as **the Pilgrim's Path** — a network of hidden, totem-marked routes meant to guide these newly awakened Spark-bearers safely toward the Spire. Centuries later, a group of wanderers stumbled onto a fragmented, decontextualized piece of that same Warden lore, and understanding nothing of what it had actually meant, built an entire religion atop the misunderstanding. That religion survives today as the Mandate of the Chain. I record this connection not to mock them, but because I think it is a useful and humbling reminder of how easily true history becomes false doctrine, once enough of the context that made it true has been lost. It is, in its way, the central danger my entire order exists to prevent.

---

## XVIII. When the Wardens Turned on Each Other

Immortality did not make the twelve First Wardens safe from Sh'lar, whose war with this world had not ended with the Divine War's last battle. She found them, whispering into minds already worn thin by decades of guarding something that could unmake the universe if it slipped even once, and under her influence the Wardens turned on one another. Nine of the original twelve died in the civil war that followed. Because the three who survived had spilled the blood of their own kin using the very gifts the Engineer had given them, his own built-in failsafe triggered in response, and their immortality was stripped from them along with it.

Those three — remembered by the titles they earned afterward, the Architect, the Prophet, and the Mage — spent what remained of their now-mortal lives carving the beginnings of a permanent legacy directly into the Engineer's skull and torso, building what would become the Sacred Spire, my order's headquarters to this very day. The Mage in particular founded the school of sigil threading, the direct ancestor of every Elemental Conductor and every piece of Aether-Tech built since. I do not believe there is a single working piece of magical technology on this world that does not, if traced back far enough, lead to something the Mage first put into words in his final years.

The order they founded persists today as **the Grey Wardens**, publicly known — on the rare occasion they're known at all — as wandering, gray-robed ascetics, a cover that has served them well precisely because it is so unremarkable. Their oldest and wisest members undergo a final ritual, burning out their own eyes to replace them with Chaos Sight, becoming **the Circle of the Eyeless**: a fixed circle of twelve, one drawn from each of the twelve Power lineages. What binds them together is a purpose known to no one outside the Circle itself — the unlocking, eventually, of a final vault whose very existence is kept secret even from the rest of the Wardens they otherwise lead. I have tried, more than once, and failed, more than once, to learn what waits inside it, or why it apparently takes all twelve lineages standing together to open. I record the gap honestly rather than filling it with a guess I cannot support.

I should note, because I believe it matters and because I did not understand it myself until relatively late in my research, that the Grey Wardens are not alone in their inheritance. The Mandate of the Chain traces back to this same origin — the population that once lived in the sacred land surrounding the Engineer as his body petrified, imprinted with his final, fading command: *guard the prisons.* One branch of that shared ancestry kept the mission intact and became the Wardens. The other drifted, lost the context that would have let them understand what they carried, and rebuilt their inheritance into something closer to a misreading than a memory. They are not two unrelated movements that happened to stumble upon the same lost archive. They are estranged family, still, however distantly, pointed at the very same task.

---

## XIX. The Two Moons and the Rhythm of the Broken World

Two bodies orbit Ostraka now, and neither does so by accident. **Cruorbus**, the small rogue body born from the Sun God's own discarded remains, was pulled into a close, steady orbit during the Dominion's ascension ritual — the working reaching out and hauling the drifting body inward as an unintended consequence of its own violence. Its arrival is what knocked **Erranith**, the original moon and the Sun God's own watching eye, into the long, wide, erratic orbit it still follows — the reason it is the one that ended up shattered. Why it shattered has its own answer, and that answer has nothing to do with orbital mechanics. The Wolves of the Heartland hold that the Moon broke because their ancestors looked away from it for a single unforgivable moment, and an entire warrior culture has been built since around never letting that failure happen a second time. I find I have no argument sturdy enough to dismiss them, whatever my own methods might otherwise prefer.

Cruorbus, steady in its close orbit, provides Ostraka's constant background bombardment of ambient chaos — the energy that feeds the Sacred Groves continuously and drives the shorter, more familiar 49-day Meander cycle as its position shifts overhead. Erranith, erratic and wounded, provides something rarer and considerably more violent: roughly once every 169 Ostraka years, its wide orbit swings it back into close proximity, and the resulting chaos flux triggers a massive meteor storm. The debris from that storm — fragments of the shattered moon itself — is the literal source of every piece of Dragonstone ever mined. It is not, whatever certain older engineering manuals insist, "a crystallized fragment of the Celestial Clockwork." It is meteoric moon-fall, finite, rare, and fought over exactly as fiercely as something that scarce deserves to be.

Layered atop these two cycles is a third, slower rhythm, one I confess took my own order the longest to properly document. Cruorbus does not simply sit in its orbit; it librates, tilting through a slow, six-month "no" that produces four distinct seasonal phases — the Leering, the Open Eye, the Receding, and the Halo — repeated twice across a full year for eight seasons in total. When Cruorbus's scarred face points toward the surface, the leyline network runs hot and the flow shifts violently; when it turns away, the world runs comparatively calm.

Once a year, that same orbit carries Cruorbus into perfect alignment with the sun, and the world goes dark for thirteen days. This is **Shadow Week** — not the seven days certain older, superseded accounts still insist upon, and not simply a week of "the world's most violent reality-tearing," as those same accounts would have it. What actually happens is stranger, and in its own way more dangerous: the slowed chaos flow calms most of the world's ambient danger, even as it starves the twelve Sacred Groves of the steady bombardment that keeps their seals at full strength. The prisons weaken for those thirteen days. Cult activity spikes without exception, every year, as reliably as the eclipse itself. Each dragon's influence bleeds through stronger than at any other point on the calendar. The world goes quiet and dark, and something with teeth gets a little looser in its cage. I have never once, in all my years of study, found that fact anything other than genuinely unsettling, and I distrust anyone who claims they have grown entirely used to it.

## XX. What the Fae Actually Left Behind

Almost the entire Fae people made it through the Exodus portal before it closed behind them. The handful who did not were hunted down quickly and consumed as raw material for the Dominion's ascension experiments — there was no time, in the chaos of that era, for anyone left behind to hide successfully. What survives now on Erranith is a trapped, desperate people, reaching into susceptible minds across Ostraka in a continuing attempt to influence and manipulate their way back through a door that no one — not the Fae, not the Magistars, not my own order, for all our years of trying — actually knows how to reopen. That influence is real, and it is the true shape of what people generally call "the Fae con": not the invention of new religions from nothing, as I once believed myself, but the quiet infiltration of faiths that are *already real*, a false voice whispering inside a true belief, wherever a mind proves susceptible enough to let it in. Where a faction's faith traces to a genuinely present Magistar's Signature Chaos, or to a real god like the sun the Church of the Eternal Dawn worships, that faith is not a lie. It is simply, potentially, also a door the Fae are quietly trying to slip through. At least one concrete instance is known to us: an individual cult-like movement that began, quietly, at Skyroost Academy — the origin point, in fact, of the Red Sash — though it has long since outgrown that seed and taken on a life entirely its own, which I mention as a caution against assuming every Fae-touched thing remains Fae-controlled forever.

Every 169 years, when Erranith swings close enough to shower Ostraka in meteoric debris, a handful of the Barrens' most desperate and unstable Fae attempt to ride those falling fragments back down. Most landings leave nothing behind but remains — inert, but still potent enough to serve as an anchor point that strengthens the whispering influence across whatever region they fall in. The rare Fae who survives the landing arrives already broken by the attempt: a mad, warped shape of raw magic, and contact with Ostraka's own corrupted, tainted chaos only pushes them further past whatever edge they had left before the fall. These survivors are catastrophically dangerous and are dealt with quickly by whoever or whatever happens to be nearby, my own order's people more often than I would like. None of these encounters are ever officially recorded, by design as much as by circumstance. They persist only as a region's own whispered legends and ghost stories — which is, I believe, exactly why so many of Ostraka's separate, unconnected cultures share the same shape of "haunted meteor crash" folklore without any of them realizing they are all describing the very same thing. I have begun, quietly, to cross-reference these stories region by region. The pattern, once you know to look for it, is difficult to unsee.

## XXI. What Sh'lar Actually Is, Now

Sh'lar does not sit somewhere issuing orders to a network of loyal handlers, whatever the more dramatic accounts would have you believe. She is diffuse now, ambient, thoroughly and irreversibly mad. Functionally, she has become something closer to an emotion than a person: she *is* the whisper of paranoia, the voice in a mind that insists everyone is against them, that no one's words can be trusted, turning people against one another one susceptible thought at a time. She points what she touches toward the Worm Cults, not through instruction so much as inclination — every whisper she makes serves the same single goal, which is disrupting the contained flow of chaos badly enough to eventually break a prison open. What looks, from the outside, like coordination between separate cults is, as best my order has been able to determine, really just the same poison seeded independently into thousands of separate minds, arriving at similar conclusions because it was always built to arrive there.

## XXII. Speaking to a God in a Bottle

It is, in fact, possible to hear a Magistar's voice directly — a mind that gets close enough, or proves susceptible enough, can receive real contact, real visions, real warnings pulled from something far older and more knowing than any mortal source could ever offer. I want to be clear that this is not a gift, whatever it might feel like to the person receiving it. Every Magistar, without a single documented exception, is completely and irrecoverably mad. Each is chaos itself trapped in a bottle, and if any one of them were released uncontained, even for a moment, it would unmake the universe around it entirely. Direct contact with a mind like that routinely drives the listener mad in turn. When Fane the Gambler received his vision of the coming Shattering, it was real, it was accurate, and it very likely cost him something in the receiving of it that he never got back. A warning from a Magistar is, without exception in every case I have documented, both of those things at once: true, and dangerous to have heard. I record this last, deliberately, because I want it to be the thing you remember longest. Logic is my shield. It has never once claimed to be a guarantee of safety. Only a way of seeing the danger clearly enough to survive it.

*— C.*
