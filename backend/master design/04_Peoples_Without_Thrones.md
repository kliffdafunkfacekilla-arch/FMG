# Ostraka: Peoples Without Thrones
### Compiled from the notes of Clary, First Archivist of the Order of the Clockwork

*Part of the Ostraka canon reference set. Fourth volume, covering nations and peoples whose power does not rest on the same foundations as the Great Powers — forced republics, hive-minds, migrating cities, and cultures built entirely around a wound rather than a throne.*

---

### A Note on the Word "Lesser"

I want to head off an assumption before it takes root, because I have watched it take root in other people's writing and I do not want to repeat their mistake. Nothing in this volume is here because it matters less than what I wrote about in the previous one. The eight peoples that follow simply do not organize themselves the way a Great Power does — some have no fixed capital at all, some are ruled by something closer to a single collective mind than a government, and at least one exists only because its enemies bred it into existence on purpose. I have found, if anything, that the strangest governance produces the most instructive history. Read accordingly.

## I. The Sump-Kin — Toad Barons and Chem-Barons

I do not think any nation on Ostraka does more necessary, more thankless work than the Sump-Kin, and I do not think any nation is more quietly resented for it either.

Theirs is the labor no one else wants named directly: they process the raw sludge and toxic industrial waste of half the continent, and in doing so they are the actual mechanism behind what I have come to call, in my own private notes, the Geopolitical Hypocrisy — both the Coastal Theocracy and the Heartland Alliance buy their refined goods through River Folk middlemen precisely so their own hands can stay clean of the processing that makes those goods usable in the first place. The Sump-Kin know this. I have never once heard one of them pretend otherwise.

Their patron is Gavusrix, the Quicksilver Mother, and the Toad Barons who lead them have convinced themselves they can "farm the dragon" — pumping sludge directly into her prison's ecosystem in hopes of harvesting whatever mutated biological wealth comes back out. I want to record plainly that I consider this one of the more reckless gambles I have documented anywhere on this continent. Her domain is vitality and biomancy, uncontrolled life given divine scale, and cultivating monstrous abundance deliberately, at the very edge of a seal that could fail on any given Shadow Week, strikes me as a wager the Sump-Kin are one bad year away from losing badly.

During Shadow Week itself, River Folk families gather on their own toxic waters and float thousands of lanterns across the darkness, staying awake the full length of the eclipse to tend them. I want to be clear that this is not simply ceremony, however beautiful I found it the one time I was permitted to witness it firsthand. A gap in the lantern chain is a real, practical signal — an upstream watch-post has failed, and help is needed before the gap widens. It is a culture that has built its most sacred ritual directly out of its most practical survival need, and I have come to think that combination is rarer, and more admirable, than either alone.

I want to close with the Order of Purifiers, whom I have never managed to interview directly and suspect I never will. They are a secret sect within River Folk society, composed exclusively of albino-born individuals taken from their families at birth, and their place in Sump-Kin culture is genuinely paradoxical: unspoken of, their presence quietly unsettling to almost everyone around them, and yet treated with enough reverence that failing to offer them tribute is considered a serious misstep for any trader hoping to prosper. They are, in the truest sense I have encountered anywhere, specters within their own society — seen constantly, acknowledged never.

## II. The Hive Commonwealth

I have stood inside the Quartz-Vein Corridor exactly once, and I do not intend to return without an escort I trust completely, because the Hive Commonwealth is the only place on Ostraka where I have felt, physically, the presence of a mind that was not my own brushing against the edges of my thoughts.

They are defined by a literal telepathic Hive Mind — not a metaphor for good cooperation, but a genuine, collective psychic architecture that functions as what their own scholars call a Grounded Circuit, anchoring every member against the ambient chaos of the surrounding Abyssal Frontier. Their patron, Tyrustis, sits over Lex — willpower — and I have come to think the pairing is almost too fitting: Lithium, his element, is the one conductor known to stop Psychic effects outright, which makes the sustained assault their patron's own cult wages against their collective identity a kind of grim, mechanically precise poetry. A hive-mind under psychic siege, defended by the one metal built to resist exactly that kind of attack. I do not think this is coincidence. I am not certain, yet, what it is instead.

## III. The Vaneer Concord — The Widow's Web

I want to begin this section with a confession: I chose the title "The Widow's Web" for my own working files on the Concord years before I confirmed it was literally true, and I have never forgiven myself the sloppiness of getting the right answer for entirely the wrong reason.

The Concord is not, whatever its own official histories would prefer you believe, a triumph of natural logic freely chosen by a rational people. It is the result of a continent-spanning hostage situation, engineered — deliberately, patiently, and with a coldness I still find difficult to fully comprehend — by the Widow Spiders, who bred chaos on purpose across the Southern Cratered Wastelands until the disparate insectoid clans of the region had no option left but unification. I have recovered the Concord's own unredacted intelligence records confirming this outright, which is more than I can say for most claims of this magnitude. The Webweaver Wars ran for centuries before the Widow Spiders' engineered crisis finally forced the Misfit Army and the resulting Forced Republic into being. Everything about Vaneer culture — the audits, the redundancy, the obsessive documentation — is downstream of a people who were, quite literally, bred into logic by weaponized disorder rather than choosing it freely.

Their government reflects that origin honestly, once you know to look. An elected Senate, with a strong and consistent tendency toward female representation — Ant Queens, female Mantises, rooted in a biological precedent no one in the Concord finds worth remarking on — elects a Triumvirate to lead three branches: Finance and Trade, Military and Defense, and Civil, which also happen to form the basis of the Concord's three competing political parties. The Widow Spiders themselves hold barely any direct Senate seats — often just the one — and yet consistently secure a Triumvirate chair through backroom alliance-building alone. They are, as I understand it now, the true and mostly invisible architects of Concord policy, exactly as their founding role would predict.

Society divides by species rather than by class, and I want to record the Concord's own framing here rather than my own paraphrase, because I think it matters that they say this about themselves without apparent irony: each species functions as a vital organ within the larger social body. Spiders lead in business, trade, and military intelligence. Ants form the disciplined infantry and civil-engineering workforce. Mantises and Scorpions specialize in generalship, special operations, and siege-breaking. Beetles manage logistics and engineering coordination. It is presented, and largely functions, as biological specialization rather than prejudice, though I confess I have my doubts about how comfortable that framing would sound to an Ant born without any particular gift for infantry discipline.

The Chaos Corps — the Deconstructors — strike me as the single cleverest piece of Vaneer military doctrine I have encountered anywhere on this continent, and I want to explain why, because on its face it sounds like the Concord's one moment of irrationality. It is not. It is, per their own strategic reasoning, the ultimate expression of the same cold logic that runs everything else in their society, not a contradiction of it: a unit that fights chaos by wielding it directly. They draw their ranks from the Chaos Touched — nonconformist Ants, destructive Beetles, anyone too fundamentally illogical to integrate elsewhere — sequestered rather than punished, since ordinary coexistence simply isn't possible for them. Service is for life; the Concord's own grim shorthand is that retirement is death. Command falls to whoever has survived the longest, the Veteran-Primus, a meritocracy of pure survival immune to the usual politics of rank. Their actual doctrine in the field is as clever as their recruitment: run headlong into a Chaos Zone and generate so much rapid, self-created disruption that the zone's own chaotic effects lose any stable target to latch onto. Fighting chaos with faster chaos. Their training exercises evolved, over generations, into the Gauntlet and Keystone, now the Concord's two national sports — a detail I find almost unbearably fitting for a culture this thoroughly logical about even its own entertainment.

Shadow Week arrives among the Vaneer not as crisis but as scheduled procedure — the Great Audit, a full lockdown behind military shield-walls and a complete inventory of every stockpile in the republic. I want to note, because I think it is the single most human detail in this entire account, that it is also, unofficially, the one sanctioned window each year to quietly enjoy whatever mislabeled or un-inventoried goods happen to turn up. Even the most rigidly audited culture I have ever documented has found itself one small, logical loophole for pleasure.

I will close with the Concord's own acknowledged weakness, because I respect them more for admitting it than I would if they'd pretended otherwise: their entire system assumes every internal actor behaves logically. It is a real vulnerability, and their own analysts know it as well as I do.

## IV. The Eastern Hounds (Ash-Walkers) of the Howling Steppes

I have spent more sleepless nights turning the Eastern Hounds over in my mind than I have any other people in this volume, because their entire culture is a direct, unbroken response to living beside a wound in time itself.

Their patron is Termhill, the Black Star, and the Howling Steppes are a place where — I record the old phrase exactly as I found it, because I have never improved on it — "the temporal gears of the Celestial Clockwork have slipped their cogs." It is a permanent, localized failure in the fabric of sequence itself, and survival there requires something I still find almost impossible to imagine choosing deliberately: abandoning long-term planning, and abandoning memory along with it, because memory becomes a liability that anchors the mind to traumatic echoes that are never fully in the past to begin with. Where the Ursine build a culture around remembering their failures and the Scute build one around vengeance carried forward for generations, the Hounds have built theirs around strategic, deliberate forgetting — and I want to record, honestly, that I no longer think this makes them weaker than either. It makes them different, shaped by a wholly different flavor of the same broken world.

Generations of psychic assault have taught them never to trust their eyes alone — the Black Star's influence works through illusion and phantoms of the past, and their culture has adapted by leaning hard on every other sense a body possesses. Their communal chanting, drumming, and droning is not, whatever an outsider might assume on first hearing it, entertainment. It is a deliberate psycho-acoustic bulwark, meant quite literally to drown out the whispers and fortify the pack's collective mind against them.

Their relationship with their own dead carries a paradox I did not understand until I'd spent considerable time among them: a people who fear the past this completely also maintain a deeply shamanistic connection to their ancestors, and the two are not, in the end, in tension at all. Respecting the dead is treated as active defense — an improperly honored spirit becomes a tool the Black Star can use to haunt the living, and "letting your grandfather haunt the halls" ranks among the gravest insults available in their language. When a cub is recognized as grave-touched, their shaman leaves them alone at the start of Shadow Week to face an ordeal no one else witnesses, sometimes for the better part of a year, and I want to note that this timing is not tradition for its own sake. It is exactly the week when Termhill's containment weakens most and his influence bleeds through strongest. The ordeal is a genuine confrontation with the dragon's power, not a ritual performed at a safe remove from it.

Once each year, every nomadic pack of the Howling Steppes converges on a single site for the Roaring Fire — bonfires visible for miles, drumming that carries further still. I have come to understand this as a deliberate act of collective defiance rather than mere celebration: a yearly declaration, made as loudly as an entire people can manage, that the Hounds are still here despite everything the Haunting has taken from them.

## V. The Sylvan Empire — Arbor-Prime, the Walking City

I want to open by correcting an error I made myself, in an earlier draft of this very volume, because I think the correction matters more than the original mistake did. I once believed the Sylvan Empire answered to Lophex. They do not. Lophex belongs, fully and confirmed, to the Flower Valley Folk, whom I write about next — and the true answer, once I finally found it, struck me as considerably better than the one I'd guessed at.

The Sylvan Empire and the Flower Valley Folk share a single origin, the Schism of the Scar, dating to roughly 250 A.W.: one united botanical civilization, splitting apart when Vecelo's sonic chaos intensified beyond what any single strategy could answer. The Sylvan Elders chose to uproot entirely and keep moving forever rather than fight or settle anywhere Vecelo's Silent Echo might reach them again. A splinter group chose the opposite — rooting themselves permanently at the Engineer's own Legs, taking up his final, fading duty as their own divine mandate. Neither side has ever fully forgiven the other's choice. The Sylvans see the Flower Folk as having surrendered their freedom for safety; the Flower Folk see the Sylvans as having run rather than stood their ground. I have heard both arguments made with equal conviction and have never found myself able to declare either one wrong.

Because the Sylvan Empire never stopped migrating, they never settled near a new Grove to inherit a patron from at all — which is, I have come to believe, exactly why their territory today picks up diffuse, secondary influence from whichever flow zones they happen to be passing through, Stagus and Tiraton both bleeding faintly into their current stretch of land, rather than answering to any single fixed dragon of their own. Vecelo remains their defining trauma. He is not, and has not been for a very long time, their patron — that belongs, uncontested, to the Heartland Alliance.

Arbor-Prime is not a city that was built. It is a city that migrates — a collection of the Empire's greatest Ancients, living, ambulatory trees and tree-bound beings, drifting at a glacial pace that has nonetheless never once stopped in all the centuries since the Schism. Their industry, which they call Dendro-Engineering, reflects that same philosophy at every scale: where the Ironborn and the Canopy Clans both hold, in their different ways, that to create is to destroy — melting ore, felling timber — the Sylvans grow their tools with patient intent instead, living Iron-Wood and sap-vein constructs that grow *stronger* with age rather than decaying, a quiet, biological rebuttal to Aurgenas's Flux-Rot that I find genuinely elegant every time I consider it.

I want to note one loose thread honestly rather than smooth it over: a single source I've recovered gives their core philosophy as "Speed is a brittle cage. We do not run, for to run is to be chased. We stand, and the storm breaks against us" — a stand-and-endure creed flatly at odds with everything else I have confirmed about a people whose entire identity is built on never stopping. I believe this is simply an error in that particular record, and I have chosen to trust the overwhelming weight of corroborating evidence over the single outlier. I record the outlier anyway, because I would rather a future archivist find my doubt already written down than assume I never noticed the contradiction at all.

## VI. The Flower Valley Folk — The Carefree Warriors

If the Sylvan Empire is defined by never stopping, the Flower Valley Folk are defined by having stopped in the single most sacred location available to anyone on this continent: the sheltered, U-shaped cradle formed by the outspread legs and knees of the Engineer himself.

Their patron is Lophex, the Geometric Void, confirmed directly and repeatedly enough that I no longer entertain the alternate name I once saw attached to them in error. Their founding mandate — "guard the prisons" — is the same final, fading command the Engineer left behind for the First Wardens, taken up here by an entirely different lineage descended from the same original Schism I described above.

Their culture runs on a philosophy I have found genuinely unsettling to witness up close: defense and hospitality treated as a single unified art, "never trust your eyes" applied not as paranoia but as polished, practiced diplomacy. A rigid three-caste system organizes them — the Blossom Caste rules through high intrigue, the Shrub Caste maintains a bureaucracy built on silence, and the Vine Caste stands as a stoic, ever-watching vanguard. Their settlements are arranged deliberately into orderly garden groups specifically to prevent surprise, and their coming-of-age rituals double, without exception, as intelligence-gathering exercises for the young people passing through them.

I want to be direct about something I did not appreciate on my first visit: their tourism and diplomacy are not separate from their espionage. They are the delivery mechanism for it. Wine, theater, and the genuinely warm hospitality of their resident Mushroom-Folk draw in the wealthiest and loosest-lipped guests on Ostraka, while the Blossoms quietly map every weakness on display, the Shrubs bury the resulting ledgers where no rival will ever find them, and the Vines watch the whole performance unfold from the cliffs above. I have come to suspect that every kindness I was shown during my own stay there was, in some measure, professional.

Several smaller peoples live under Flower Valley jurisdiction without direct political voice, trading self-governance for specialized trade in return. The Ailurus — Red Pandas, called locally the Mist-Walkers — serve as tea-masters, spies, and chemical agriculturalists from the misty bamboo district of the Lantern-Bough, running both the Coolant Cartel and their own military arm, the Fog-Wardens; I have already documented their coolant trade with the Iron Caldera elsewhere in this collection, and I note here that it is the same people, seen from a different angle. The Bradypod — Sloth-Folk, the Time-Keepers — work as textile masters, archivists, and spider-ranchers from the Slow-Vault, a fortress-tree of web and moss where every transaction moves at a pace I found, on my one visit, genuinely difficult to adjust to. I want to note that this is the second confirmed Bradypod population I have documented — the other lives among the Avians at the Fulcrum Bank — and I have come to see this kind of divided population as simply how a small, specialized people survives on Ostraka: rooted in more than one nation at once, belonging fully to neither.

## VII. The Guerrilla Clans — The Unseen Hand

I want to open by admitting that the Guerrilla Clans are, by a wide margin, the hardest people in this entire volume to actually document, which I have come to understand is precisely the point.

They answer to no patron dragon — their identity is entirely self-made, born from small folk, feline and rabbit and rodent kin, who grew tired of being trampled underfoot by larger armies and retreated instead into what they call the deep shadows, building an entire culture around disappearance rather than confrontation. Five specialized Clans make up their structure — the Iron-Briars, the Crescent-Wings, the Crimson Carapace, the Honorable Shadows, and the Knot-Cloak Dragoons — operating from Subterranean Canopy Vaults, Deep Shadow Ridges, and Camouflaged Valleys that I have never once managed to locate on my own without a guide who wished to be found.

Their doctrine, the Unseen Hand, is built on patience rather than force: study an adversary's patterns and weaknesses at length before ever engaging directly. I find the clearest illustration of this in an incident I have documented at greater length elsewhere — when a stolen skiff violated Clan airspace, they did not kill the trespassers who'd wandered into it. They disabled the ship, brought it down gently, and let the whole encounter unfold into what became, eventually, the founding story of the Ghostwind Raiders. Their No-Fly Zone is respected even by the Free Sky-Baronies, who I am told learned the boundaries of Clan patience the hard way and have not tested them since.

Their technology deliberately rejects the loud, volatile Aetheric-Reaction engines everyone else on this continent builds around, favoring instead single-use charms painted by their own hedge-wizards — a Leap-Charm for a silent fifty-foot jump, a Pop-Bolt for a non-lethal concussive burst that stuns rather than kills. Their economy runs the same way: a decentralized Charm Economy built on barter and Trade-Charms rather than hoarded gold, practical goods — bread, linen, wine, tools — exchanged directly, since a people this small, this mobile, and this deliberately unseen has no real use for wealth they cannot carry.

## VIII. The Prism-Scale Collective

I want to close this volume with the people I have found the most difficult to write about honestly, because everything about them is, on its surface, delightful, and everything underneath that surface disturbed me more than almost anything else I have documented in my career.

The Prism-Scale Collective is a lizard-folk society — Crystal Lizards and Komodo Guards prominent among them — organized entirely around a single obsession: attention and light. Being seen, being heard, being genuinely admired is not, in their culture, a pleasant social benefit. It is the primary measure of a person's worth, full stop. Every citizen is, functionally, a performer competing in a zero-sum contest for the spotlight, and to be ignored, uninteresting, or — in their own damning word for it — dull, is treated as close to the gravest sin their society recognizes.

Four distinct biological castes fill the roles this culture demands: the Anoles serve as virtuosos, the Chameleons as living canvases, the Geckos as climbers, and the Komodo Dragons provide what they themselves call the bass line beneath the performance. It is, on the surface, a genuinely joyful arrangement, and I watched a public performance once that I still think about with real fondness.

What sits beneath that surface is the reason I have found myself unable to write about them lightly. Their justice for failing to shine is not execution, and it is not imprisonment. It is the total, deliberate erasure of identity — the offender broken down systematically until nothing of who they were remains, then rebuilt entirely as a scarred, unwaveringly loyal bodyguard assigned to protect the very noble houses that judged them unworthy of the spotlight in the first place. It is a closed loop, and I want to be precise about what that means: it removes every non-conformist the Collective produces, and it manufactures elite enforcers from the identical process, with nothing wasted at any stage. Every dazzling performance I have ever watched in Prism-Scale territory was shadowed, I now understand, by exactly these broken, silently loyal figures — a living reminder, present at every single celebration, of precisely what happens to anyone who ever stops shining.

*— C.*
