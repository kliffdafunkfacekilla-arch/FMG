# Ostraka: Outlaws & Sky Powers
### Compiled from the notes of Clary, First Archivist of the Order of the Clockwork

*Part of the Ostraka canon reference set. Fifth volume, covering four powers whose territory is not land but the open sky between it — and whose relationship to law, honesty, and violence could not be more different from one another despite how easily an outsider might lump them together.*

---

### A Note on Not Confusing Them

I want to open with a warning I wish someone had given me before my first year in this work, because I confused two of the following four factions in an early report and the resulting misunderstanding nearly got a contact of mine killed. All four of the peoples in this volume fly. All four operate, in one sense or another, outside the reach of a Great Power's law. That is where the resemblance ends. One is a mercenary company that will hold a contract to the letter and never once break it. One is a union of outlaw haulers who mostly just want to be left alone to move goods without the banks taking a cut. One is a deniable weapon dressed up as a folk hero. And one is not really a nation at all, but a fleet of desperate specialists who've made a home of the one stretch of sky nobody else wants. Do not mistake any of them for one another. I did, once. I do not intend to again.

---

## I. The Ivory-Gate Syndicate

I want to begin with the one whose reputation is, against every expectation I carried into my research, the most honest of the four.

They were not always a fleet, and they were not always feared. Two peoples shared the Ivory Pass once in a quiet, informal symbiosis that predated any of the trouble that followed — Caprine goat-herders and Ceratomorph rhino-keepers, living alongside one another with no particular need for either flags or weapons. Aether-Skiff technology ended that peace the moment it turned their mountain pass into a commercial shortcut. Corporate crews and opportunistic Corsair pirates began treating the locals as beasts of burden rather than neighbors, dumping waste through the pass and taking potshots for sport, until one crew went further than even that and desecrated a birthing ground, killing a Goat elder in the process. The resulting war-party did not simply retaliate. They beat the offending crew half to death and dismantled their ship entirely — and when the locals tried, afterward, to formalize a toll for safe passage through territory that was, by any reasonable measure, theirs, the corporate and Sky-Baron fleets crushed the attempt by force rather than negotiate.

That was the last mistake anyone made against them for a very long time. The natives ambushed and captured an entire heavily escorted trade convoy at the pass's narrowest point, and built, from what they took, the Bone-Toll Towers — the remains of anyone foolish enough to run the blockade, displayed openly as a warning that has apparently never needed repeating.

What survives today is a globally deployed toll authority and mercenary fleet, no longer confined to the canyon that made them. Their doctrine is the simplest and, I have come to think, the most trustworthy of any faction I've documented in this entire collection: pay, and you receive an Ivory Chit — carved, pointedly, from the bones of the corporate fleets that first tried to conquer them — along with passage no one else on Ostraka can guarantee as reliably. Refuse, and you receive nothing at all, not even the courtesy of violence. As the saying goes among their own crews, they don't kill you. They just refuse to anchor you. They let the sky have you.

Their day-to-day enforcement, I have learned, runs on something closer to calculated legal theater than pure brutality — a multi-stage interception process built to make surrender feel financially and legally inevitable before a single shot is fired, their War Skiffs functioning as much like floating legal offices backed by overwhelming firepower as they do like warships in the conventional sense. I do not consider this a contradiction of the Bone-Toll Towers' brutal deterrence. I consider it the same organization's professional, everyday face — contracts and statutes first, and the display of remains only once the paperwork has already failed.

They are, by their own written code, genuinely faithful to a contract's terms for as long as it remains valid. I have hired them myself, once, for a research expedition I would rather not detail here, and I want to record plainly that they held to the letter of our agreement in circumstances where I fully expected them not to.

## II. The Free Sky-Baronies

Where Ivory-Gate is defined by the contract, the Free Sky-Baronies are defined by their refusal to sign one at all.

I want to correct a misconception first, because I held it myself for longer than I should have: they are explicitly not a Robin Hood faction — that reputation belongs, as I document elsewhere in this collection, to the Ghostwind Raiders — and they are not, strictly, pirates either, whatever the "outlaw" label might suggest to an outsider. What they actually are is closer to a gritty, blue-collar motorcycle club scaled up to the size of a federation: union haulers who turned to organized crime because it was the only path left to survival, rather than knights-errant looking for a cause. Their doctrine, the Sovereignty of the Skiff, rejects every flavor of what they call Dry-Land Law — Avian, Vaneer, Scute, it makes no difference to them — on the plain belief that the sky cannot belong to anyone who only wants to tax it.

Twelve Barons lead the Syndicate, each controlling a Chapter built around a trade route near a specific Sacred Grove, which I suspect gives every Chapter its own regional dragon-flavor even though the Syndicate as a whole answers to no single patron. Their territory is not fixed settlement at all but Tethered Free-Yards and Drift-Slums, massive lashed-together flotillas hidden deep in high-altitude drift currents, operating under what they themselves call the Ghost Flotilla strategy — not a trackable standing fleet anyone could hope to besiege, but a network of hidden, temporary, deliberately camouflaged bases kept well away from any controlled port. Their initiation, Taking the Burn, is not a symbolic patch sewn onto a jacket the way an outsider might expect from the "outlaw club" comparison. It is a physical, bio-mechanical branding rite — the Core-Brand — and I have come to understand that among the Baronies, this distinction matters enormously; a life-and-hull commitment, not a symbol of one.

Born from what their own histories call the Hauler's Rebellion, their economy today runs on what they call Liquid Gold — functionally, the Prohibition Kings of the Shatterlands, a monopoly-and-laundering operation I suspect touches more of the continent's illegal trade than any single Great Power would care to admit publicly. I want to distinguish them clearly, one final time, from Ivory-Gate: the Baronies are anti-enforcement rather than pro-hire. They will disrupt the Gilded Compass's operations opportunistically when the chance arises, but they exist, at their core, to move goods and people through the skies while avoiding the Fulcrum Bank's iron economic rule entirely — an alternate shipping network built on defiance, not a mercenary company built on reliability.

## III. The Ghostwind Raiders

I have saved the most carefully constructed lie in this entire volume for the middle of the chapter, because I think it deserves to be understood in relation to both of its neighbors before I explain what it actually is.

Publicly, the Ghostwind Raiders are a pirate consortium — honorable ones, by every account that reaches the public ear, hurting the powerful and spreading their gains to the small and vulnerable, functioning as a strike unit against whichever regional power has accumulated too much control. I want to be direct about something I confirmed only after considerable difficulty: this reputation is genuinely deserved. It is not empty propaganda. And it is also, simultaneously, exactly the cover story a deniable black-ops fleet would need to operate freely across contested territory without anyone asking the wrong questions.

There is no "phantom fleet." There has never been a fleet at all — a single ship, classified internally under the designation Ghost-Link, crewed by a cell of the Guerrilla Clans, jointly funded off the official books by two parties who present themselves publicly as unrelated: the Avian Empire's Gilded Compass, seeking plausible deniability while disrupting rival trade, and the Meridian Chain's Hexarchy, funding black operations and asset denial while maintaining an untouched public face of "Mutual Profit." A named handler, Grand-Link Nero, oversees the arrangement from whatever remove keeps both funding parties comfortably unable to answer for it if questioned. Their actual mandate breaks into three tactical priorities: Surgical Extraction, seizing valuable Aether-Tech and Dragonstone from contested corridors before a rival can; Systemic Sabotage, disguised carefully as natural Meander shifts rather than deliberate attack; and Groves Reconnaissance, mapping the imprisoned Magistars' Signature Chaos on the Meridian Chain's behalf.

I want to record what I consider the most elegant piece of statecraft in this entire chapter: both the Chain and the Compass are running the exact same deniable proxy through the same single ship, each apparently unaware — or, more likely, each choosing not to notice — that the other is doing precisely the same thing. I do not think this is an oversight worth correcting in any future account. I think it is the joke the whole arrangement is quietly built on, and I have come to enjoy it rather more than I probably should.

## IV. The Dust-Husk Riders

I want to close this volume with the one people among these four who are not, by any honest measure, outlaws at all — simply survivors of a stretch of sky so hostile that no other faction has ever successfully claimed it.

They are not a single species, and I think that distinction matters more for the Riders than for anyone else in this collection. They are a genuine symbiotic fleet, a confederation of radically different plant-like peoples, each contributing a specialized biological role rather than sharing one uniform culture, nomads of what everyone else on Ostraka simply calls the Dust Bowl — a region hostile enough that it stands as a No-Fly Zone even for factions who fear very little else. I have come to believe their diversity is not merely a curiosity of their culture but their entire strategic advantage: no single species could perceive, let alone survive, what the Dust Bowl demands, and it took all of them together to manage it.

They are, so far as I have been able to confirm, the primary harvesters of the Lunar Ghost-Blooms — the source of Aether-Clear, the cosmetic Void-Cream, and the narcotic known simply as the 4-Year Seed. It is dangerous, patient, unglamorous work, conducted in a place most of Ostraka would rather forget exists at all, and I have come to think the Riders prefer it that way. A people this well-suited to being overlooked rarely mind the anonymity. It has, after all, kept them alive this long.

*— C.*
