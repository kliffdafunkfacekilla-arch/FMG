# Ostraka: The Underworld & Secret Orders
### Compiled from the notes of Clary, First Archivist of the Order of the Clockwork

*Part of the Ostraka canon reference set. Sixth volume, covering the powers that operate beneath every map a Great Power would show you — including, I will admit with some discomfort, my own.*

---

### A Note on Writing About Myself

I have avoided, everywhere else in this collection, writing about the order I belong to with anything resembling objectivity, and I do not intend to pretend otherwise here. I will tell you what I know to be true, and I will tell you plainly where my own loyalty makes me the wrong person to judge it fairly. Read this section with that in mind. I have tried to write it the way I would want a stranger's account of my own order to be written — honestly, but not innocently.

---

## I. The Order of the Clockwork — The Religion of Science

We are not a nation, and we have never wanted to be one. Our actual purpose — the thing every Rudder, every Forge, every cell scattered across this continent ultimately serves — is the restoration of reality itself: gathering, patiently and across generations if necessary, whatever knowledge might one day let someone undo the damage the Shattering left behind. I want to be clear that our secrecy is not paranoia dressed up as principle. It is protection for the Great Work against exactly the kind of short-lived, self-interested government that would misuse it the instant it proved useful for anything less than what it was built for. I keep our founder's own words in my private ledger, and I will share them here, because I think they say more than anything I could write myself: we are builders of a cage we pray stays empty.

We were founded by the crew of the Write Wager — six specialists, remembered collectively as the Hexad Pillars, our founding myth and our origin both at once. I will not name them all again here; I have written of them at length elsewhere. What matters for this account is how the Order actually operates today, which is smaller and stranger than our founding legend might suggest. Cells run on what we call the Pentad Protocol — five fixed roles, the Rudder as tactical leader and conscience, alongside the Forge, the Archive, the Veil, and the Hearth. Every member of a cell holds an absolute, non-negotiable Veto Power, the right to freeze a cell outright the instant it shows signs of what we call Magistar Syndrome — the particular, familiar hubris of believing that enough knowledge justifies acting alone, without oversight, without restraint. I have used that veto exactly once in my own career. I do not regret it, and I have never spoken publicly of what it prevented.

Membership is never sought. It is earned, slowly, through a filtration process that can take years without the candidate ever knowing they're being evaluated. Once a year, six Legacy Guilds gather at a neutral site — traditionally the Aether-Spire — for what looks, to any outside observer, like routine trade arbitration. Somewhere on the grounds sits an object that has resisted explanation for generations: a monument, a broken steam-regulator, a mural no one has ever fully decoded. A candidate who has spent years quietly obsessing over their own fragment of that puzzle, guided there without their knowledge by our own agents embedded as ordinary artisans, eventually finds five strangers at the same gathering who share the same obsession. When all six Personal Treasures are finally brought together, the object yields a hidden route to what we call the Clockwork Cache, and six people quietly vanish from their Guild's registries, never to be missed by anyone who wasn't meant to notice.

I want to record the darkest part of our own structure honestly, because I think an archive that flatters its author is worthless. Every cell believes their Rudder is simply their leader. None of them know that their base, or their airship, is rigged with a Dead Man's Switch — a volatile Sump-Ignis charge that only the Rudder can defuse, and which will, if the Rudder dies, disappears, or fails to reset it in time, kill the entire cell without exception. It is a hidden execution mechanism built into the very architecture of trust we ask our members to have in one another. The Rudder lives beside people they know, with absolute certainty, they might one day be forced to kill. I have carried that weight myself. I do not recommend it to anyone who has a choice.

Even the Rudder is not fully trusted, in the end. The Reliance — Mushroom Folk, immortal and unnervingly stoic — installed every Dead Man's Switch in the first place, and monitor the Order's global temperament without interfering in day-to-day work, unless a cell oversteps badly enough to warrant it. When that happens, they trigger a total blackout and send Collectors to retrieve whatever's left. I have come to think of the chain of trust within my own order as something like a ladder with a gap built into the last rung on purpose: the Crew trusts the Rudder, the Rudder trusts the Code, and the Reliance trusts no one at all. For members whose knowledge has become too valuable to lose but whose judgment has failed beyond recovery, we maintain the Gilded Cage — a subterranean prison beneath the University of Reliance, hidden in the ruins of a city most maps have forgotten. Cells there are opulent, chaos-shielded, and permanent. A double-gate, two-key system ensures that no single person, however senior, can ever free an occupant alone. I have visited it exactly once. I do not intend to return.

## II. The Obsidian Cartel and the Silk-Sovereign Syndicate

I want to correct, plainly and without hedging, an error I made myself in an earlier draft of this collection: the Obsidian Cartel is not my own order under a different name. I believed this for longer than I should have, and I record the correction here because I think an honest archive owes its reader that much.

They are a genuinely separate underworld power, operating in many of the same shadowed spaces my own order moves through, guided by their own mission rather than ours: *in a world held together by tension, we are the friction that prevents the slip.* Three pillars structure everything they do — Stability, preventing monopolies that risk pushing reality itself toward failure; Secrecy, clandestine movement through No-Fly Zones and the most heavily surveilled Citadels on the continent; and Leverage, controlling the flow of Technical Truths tightly enough to keep every surface power locked in mutual, cynical dependence on one another.

The Silk-Sovereign Syndicate grew from within the Cartel's own ecosystem, born from specialized laborers who broke free of exploitation under the Verdant Tangle's Twelve Crowns and the Sump-Kin's refineries — and who then, with a boldness I find myself admiring despite everything else I know about them, turned the Cartel's own methodology back around to win total sovereignty for themselves. Their worker-safety protocol, the Mandate of Iron-Weave, runs through a Shadow-Set Contract administered by Ink-Wrights, opening with the Pre-Ink Council — a multi-day negotiation in which the Ink-Wright psychically verifies that both parties share genuinely identical intent before the ritual is ever permitted to proceed. I have written elsewhere about the real personal risk an Ink-Wright carries in doing this honestly; I will only add here that becoming one means training in Chaos Weaving, Biomancy, and Jurisprudence all at once, and staking one's own body against every contract witnessed. A minor breach triggers the Itch, a distracting psychological static; a major breach triggers the Warp, Chaos Corruption withering the limb entirely. Within Syndicate territory, a person's collection of Silver Scars — contracts successfully fulfilled — is their entire social currency. Clean, unmarked skin reads there as untrustworthy, and commercially, as good as dead.

Their emergency infrastructure, the Stasis Sanctuary, uses Bradypod-treated symbiotic moss to slow both time and decay, wrapped in Iron-Weave Canvas woven from Weaver-Cow silk that muffles occupants from the world's ordinary physics entirely — a genuine refuge even from predators that hunt by vibration. Their intelligence network, the Web of Whispers, partners with what they call Small-Paws, Mice and Squirrel clerks who weaponize routine audits, and I want to record a connection I only confirmed after considerable cross-referencing of my own notes: this is the true, confirmed source of the Logic-Rot the Vaneer Concord names as its own great internal enemy. The Syndicate is not merely rumored to be behind it. They are.

I want to flag one further detail carefully, because I have seen it cause genuine confusion in correspondence I've received from other researchers. The Syndicate also deploys operatives they call Whisperers — disciples of Sh'lar, trained to identify the precise moment a target's willpower fails, for immediate exploitation. These are not the same Whisperers I write about elsewhere in this collection, the empathic role tied to the Flower Valley Folk that stabilizes a crew's emotional strain aboard an airship. The word is shared. The concept is not. I have made this mistake myself in earlier notes, and I correct it here so no one repeats it after me. Against Excelsis Citadel's considerable surveillance, the Syndicate relies on Flash Chromatophore Systems — signaling in colors outside the range Opecten's own sensors are built to monitor — and grades every piece of intelligence they gather from Grade I, personal vices worth leveraging quietly, up through Grade III, structural secrets on the scale of a prison seal's actual resonance data.

## III. The River Ghosts

I want to open this section with an apology to every sailor who has ever told me a ghost story and been quietly corrected by my own skepticism, because I owe them all one now. The River Ghosts are not supernatural in the least. They are a hidden society, an exiled sub-culture of River Folk, biological pariahs driven underground generations ago, now living along a hidden highway of warm mineral caves connecting the terraces of the Hive Commonwealth to the swamps of the Sump-Kin — an uncontrolled logistical corridor that, as far as I have been able to determine, no faction currently governs at all.

Cut off from the surface for so many generations, their culture has inverted entirely what the rest of us would consider ordinary. Their oral tradition treats surface-dwellers as the true monsters of the story — I am told a sleepless Vaneer Logic-Master, or an Avian fixated on some abstract concept like Truth, reads to them as an incomprehensible horror rather than the reverse. When they must surface, they adopt a beggar-merchant disguise, very likely aided by low-level Biomancy or Illusion, to mask their pale, semi-aquatic features from anyone who might otherwise recognize what they are.

Their survival strategy, what they themselves call the Haunting Doctrine, is deliberate and weaponized rather than random. The first tier is pure psychological theater — boiling springs that steam unexpectedly, disorienting echoes in the dark, a pale shape glimpsed vanishing beneath the water — enough to frighten off anyone superstitious enough to be discouraged by a ghost story. The second tier is quieter and considerably more final: an investigator who won't be deterred by theater alone is eliminated without spectacle, the body arranged afterward to resemble an accident or an animal attack, letting the region's genuine natural dangers absorb whatever suspicion might otherwise fall on the people actually responsible.

I want to record, because I believe it is true and because it concerns my own order directly, that this defense — which has held for generations against ordinary superstition — is failing against something considerably harder to fool. My own order has identified their travel patterns as a data anomaly rather than folklore, cross-referencing shipping manifests and traveler reports until the "haunting" resolved, under enough scrutiny, into an obviously real transit corridor. We do not believe in ghosts. We believe in patterns, and patterns, unlike fear, do not startle easily. I consider the coming confrontation between the Exiles' world of myth and my own order's world of data close to inevitable at this point, and I record it here not as settled history but as a live and unresolved tension I expect to be writing about again before long.

## IV. The Mandate of the Chain

I have saved this account for last because it is, in its way, the saddest story in this entire volume, and I want to give it the room that sadness deserves rather than rushing past it the way I once did in an earlier draft.

The Mandate goes by two names, and I want to be precise that both describe the same movement rather than two separate ones. Where a given cell leans grassroots and populist, emphasizing identity and resentment of Spark-based elites, it calls itself the Covenant of Null. Where a cell has organized itself into something closer to formal theocracy, it calls itself the Mandate of the Chain. Both trace to the same root: the plain biological fact that roughly half of Ostraka's population — the Null — carries no magical ability whatsoever, and both function, whatever their differences in structure, as an independent check against Spark-based elites consolidating power unchallenged, much as my own order does in its own separate way.

Their scripture, the Way of the Uncursed, was born from an accident of history I find genuinely tragic every time I revisit it. Centuries ago, wandering tribes recovered a fragmented cache of Warden lore — a technical manual on Aetheric grounding vectors, nothing more mystical than that — and, entirely lacking the context that would have let them understand what they'd actually found, mistranslated it into a religion. To the Mandate, being born Null is not biological chance. It is a state of divine purity, and magic itself is treated as pollution, to be chained and bled out of the world entirely.

Their everyday weapon, the Null-Chain, is woven from Voltaic Fleece — a kinetic sponge lethal specifically to Spark-users, the grassroots-level expression of the same grounding principle their theocratic rite formalizes at greater cost. That rite, the Rite of the First Link, admits only Null recruits, who grip a saturated iron chain link as their initiation. If a recruit secretly carries even a faint, unconfessed Spark, the resulting feedback — what the Mandate calls a Spark-Bias Blowout — either petrifies them instantly or unravels their mind into what witnesses have described to me as senseless math and crystalline lattice. The Mandate treats this horrific death as the chain purifying a liar, never as a tragedy, and I want to record, because I think it is the single darkest mechanical detail in this entire collection, that it is a direct and brutal application of a wielder's own vulnerability turned back against them — the same principle I've documented elsewhere, simply weaponized here without mercy.

Rank within the Mandate is earned by physically hauling saturated Breaker Metals to ground out each of the Twelve Powers in turn, and to rise to Grand Leader, a zealot who has already earned all twelve chains must survive a pilgrimage into the Convergence itself and return bearing a thirteenth chain, forged from raw Dragonstone — genuine meteoric debris, I should note, not the "fragment of the Celestial Clockwork" that older, superseded accounts still claim it to be.

I want to close with the detail that I find, more than any other single fact in this entire collection, impossible to set down without feeling something close to grief. The Mandate never understood the Warden archive their entire religion was built upon, and their zealotry — every chain, every purge, every recruit lost to a Blowout they believe is righteous — accidentally recharges the very Grove seals they believe themselves to be threatening. A militant order dedicated, with total sincerity, to destroying magic is, in practice and without their knowledge, helping maintain the Patch that holds this broken world together. I have written elsewhere, in my account of the Grey Wardens, that the Mandate and my own order's oldest allies share a single common ancestry — one branch that kept the mission, and one that lost the context that would have let them understand it. I do not think the Mandate are villains in any simple sense. I think they are proof of how easily a true inheritance becomes a false doctrine, once enough of what made it true has been allowed to slip away. It is, in the end, the exact danger my own order exists to prevent, and I record their story here as much as a warning to myself as to anyone who reads after me.

*— C.*
