# Ostraka: The Great Powers
### Compiled from the notes of Clary, First Archivist of the Order of the Clockwork

*Part of the Ostraka canon reference set. Third volume, covering the eight nations with enough territory, standing government, and settled population to properly be called powers in their own right.*

---

### A Note on What I Mean by "Great"

I want to be honest about the word before I use it twenty more times in this volume. I do not mean *good*. I have visited most of these nations personally, and I would not call more than two or three of them kind, and I would not call any of them entirely honest about what they are. What I mean by "great" is simply this: each of the eight nations that follow holds territory it can defend, a government that outlives any single ruler, and a population large enough that its disappearance would reshape the map. That is all. Judge them as you find them.

---

## I. The Scute Confederacy — Stone Masons of the Knife's Edge

I have never met a Scute who apologized for anything, and I have come to respect that more than I expected to when I first arrived in their archipelago half convinced I was walking into a nation of unrepentant slavers.

They were not always the ones holding the chains. The Scute — reptilian, proud, and I will say plainly, *hardened* in a way I have not encountered anywhere else on Ostraka — were once property themselves, worked to exhaustion beneath the mountain fortress of a people called the Feathered Lizards, mining copper and silver and gemstone from tunnels driven deep in pursuit of something their masters never explained to the slaves digging it out for them. What the Feathered Lizards were actually chasing, I have since confirmed, was a hidden Dragon Grove, and their greed for it proved to be their undoing: a digging crew broke through into a jeweled tunnel leading straight toward the prison itself, and the Feathered Lizard rulers, unable to resist what lay at the end of it, went to claim their prize and left their slaves chained in the dark behind them. A cataclysmic surge from the Grove collapsed the tunnels shortly after. It also, incidentally, freed the slaves left buried inside, and left them standing in the ruins beside a cache of dark glass bottles — chemical explosives, ready and waiting.

Seven of those freed slaves — remembered now as the Black Scale 7 — used what they'd found to bring the entire fortress down at sundown, collapsing the mountain itself into a jagged pit and trapping their former masters at the bottom of it. When the survivors begged for mercy, the Black Scale 7 offered them exactly one choice, and it is the choice that has defined Scute law ever since: wear the chains you once forced on us, or die in the pit. This is the Law of the Chain, and I have come to understand it as considerably more coherent than it first appears from the outside. It rests on three pillars, and I record them here exactly as I found them, because I think paraphrase would do them a disservice. First: actions, not words — by proxy, zero action is zero worth. Second: what is earned is what is paid — taking what isn't earned is theft, plainly, but giving what isn't earned is treated as the same crime, not generosity, and deception falls under this same pillar, since claiming an unearned thing for yourself or handing one to someone else amounts to the identical offense. Third, and this is the pillar that finally made the rest of their society make sense to me: no one shall be in servitude unwillingly. Every enslaved person among the Scute is offered that same original choice — the pit, or the chains — and choosing the chains over death makes the resulting servitude, by their own definition, willing. That is why they buy and sell slaves without, in their own eyes, contradicting a single one of their founding principles. The person chose it. It is a commodity they consented to, not something stolen from them. I mention this because it also explains an incident I could not make sense of for months: a merchant who once sold slaves who had never been offered that choice at all was hunted down and punished with a precision the Scute rarely bother extending to lesser crimes. It was not a trade dispute. It was blasphemy against their third pillar, and they treated it accordingly.

Their faith tracks the sun, the moon, and the eclipses that mark Shadow Week, reading elemental chaos the way another people might read weather, and their accuracy has unsettled me more than once — they reportedly predicted the great fire that swept the Heartland years before it happened. The Pit itself, formed by the fortress's original collapse, now serves double duty as gladiator arena and divination chamber, where prisoners are assigned to "Gates" and priests read the outcome, blood-splatter included, as prophecy.

Everything about how they judge the rest of us comes back to one measure: proximity to what they call the knife's edge, meaning the capacity to endure hardship without complaint. Comfort-seeking earns their open contempt — I have watched a Scute trade envoy struggle visibly not to sneer at a Vaneer bureaucrat's paperwork — while the Iron Caldera, whose own founding myth runs disturbingly parallel to theirs, earns something close to genuine respect. Their stonework survives the thermal whiplash their patron dragon inflicts on the region precisely because stone is nearly the only material that can; their sheet metal, less famous but no less important, underpins Aether-Skiffs the world over, giving them a quiet economic leverage that outlasts anyone's memory of the mountain they collapsed to earn it.

Their military rides the sky on **Cloud-Cutter Rays**, bonded through a rite called the Nest-Heist — a climber spends days on a peak projecting calm dominance rather than hiding from the creature they mean to win over, luring it in with crushed beetles or a mimicked mating call, until the Ray accepts the bond freely. I find something quietly moving in that, for a culture with such a brutal founding myth: even their war-beasts are won through patience and respect rather than force. Partnership, as their own saying goes, is harder than slavery. It is also, they insist, stronger.

## II. The Coastal Theocracy — Sovereignty of the Hum

I will admit the Coastal Theocracy is the nation I have found hardest to write about honestly, because everything true about it is also, in its way, seductive, and I do not trust my own account of a place that made me want to stay.

They live beneath the waves, ruled by something they call the Hum — a euphoric, addictive frequency that keeps the ruling castes in a state of frantic, self-policing loyalty, and I want to be precise about what that means in practice: citizens are raised to believe that reporting a neighbor for the smallest sign of "moral erosion" is the highest devotion available to them. It is a totalitarian state, and it is a totalitarian state that its own people, by and large, are genuinely happy inside, which I have found is a far more frightening combination than misery ever was.

Whale and Dolphin castes handle surveillance and policing. Crab and Lobster castes work the kelp farms and thermal vents that feed the Theocracy's industry. And the Seahorse People — small, and by any measure of raw power the least significant caste in their own nation — hold a position no one else is permitted to hold at all: they are the only sanctioned intermediaries between the Theocracy and the mainland, so central to that function that they've built an entire economic empire around it, brokering the world's most sensitive information and its most feared mercenaries through channels no other caste is allowed to touch. It is a strange kind of power, built entirely on being indispensable rather than being strong, and I confess I find it the most quietly impressive thing about the entire nation.

Not everyone stays beneath the Hum's influence willingly. Exiles who resisted it have found common cause with the native Ember-Ghouls on the volcanic islands the Theocracy dismisses as worthless — the Theocracy calls the Ghouls "Monk-eys," a slur I record here only so it can be recognized for what it is. Their joint insurgency runs an operational arm called the Devil's Choice, and from what I've been able to verify, it is already running clean, effective operations, building both funds and reputation. I do not think this insurgency is an external threat to the Theocracy so much as a symptom of it — a direct, entirely predictable consequence of what the Hum does to the people who refuse to swallow it.

During Shadow Week, when the Hum's grip weakens along with every other seal on this world, the Theocracy reinforces its massive net-walls against tides gone chaotic, and gathers at its holy geothermal vents to chant without pause — practical defense and religious devotion collapsed into the same act, born of a genuine faith that the deep will one day rise to reclaim what's owed to it.

## III. The Meridian Chain — The Ghost-Light Cartel

Where the Theocracy runs on devotion, the Meridian Chain runs on something considerably colder and, I will admit, considerably easier for me to admire on a purely intellectual level: pattern, imposed relentlessly on an ocean that would otherwise refuse to hold still for anyone.

Governed by a Hexarchy of Penguins, Walrus, Seals, and Otters under the motto "Mutual Profit as a Universal Language," the Chain controls the Blue-Route, the primary maritime corridor of the south and east, through infrastructure I have come to regard as some of the finest engineering on this world. Their Ghost-Light Network — lighthouses projecting genuine Anti-Chaos Fields — carves stable, predictable Safe Zones out of stretches of ocean that would otherwise warp without warning. Their Snail-Draught logistics move house-sized bio-engineered Abyssal Snails through storms that would flip a lesser vessel, guided by Walrus and Seal Wranglers who communicate in a private language of clicks and thumps I have never managed to learn more than a handful of words of. Their Bridge-Cities sit at every chokepoint worth holding, giving the Chain an economic stranglehold that most of its trading partners have simply learned to live inside rather than fight.

Beneath the respectable face, the Chain maintains its own clandestine arm — the Order of the Wick, which I want to distinguish clearly from my own order, since the similarity in name has caused more than one dangerous misunderstanding in my correspondence over the years. The Wick runs the Black Label Service, whose agents, called Supercargos, present as three roles at once for every sensitive cargo they escort: lawyer, diplomat, and guardian, whichever the situation calls for.

I want to note, too, a smaller and stranger thing I only uncovered by accident: an entire minor cult, the Moray-Kin, born when the Penguin caste's Depth-Chimes — ordinary warning devices meant to signal an incoming chaos surge — were mystically reinterpreted by a splinter sect of River Folk as the literal voice of a god. It is a useful reminder, I think, of how easily a warning system becomes a religion once enough generations forget what it was originally for.

## IV. The Avian Empire — The Gilded Compass

I want to open with a correction, because I got this wrong myself for the better part of a year and I do not want anyone reading this to repeat my error. "The Gilded Compass" is not simply a nickname for the Avian Empire's government. It is, precisely, a nominally independent global trade cartel — one the Empire secretly majority-owns, funds, and directs through the weight of the Fulcrum Bank and control of the mountain passes that matter most. This is, I have come to believe, the single greatest hypocrisy of Avian civilization: a people who are biologically incapable of lying have built, in total secrecy, the largest deniable shell operation on Ostraka. The Cartel calls itself the Global Watchmen, and offers a thin coat of altruism — monitoring commerce prevents wider wars, or so the reasoning goes — over what is, underneath, simple ruthless control of everyone else's profit margins.

The Empire's own culture is built on a philosophy I find genuinely admirable even as I distrust everyone who practices it: the Technical Truth. An Avian cannot lie. An Avian can, however, decline to volunteer a single word more than a question strictly demands, and their entire legal and commercial tradition — the Philosophy of Omission, codified in the Book of Omissions — is built on mastering that distinction. Their capital, Excelsis Citadel, sits governed by the High Judge of the Order of the Iron-Beak, and on that Judge's own desk sits the amber-preserved eye of Emperor Kaelos the Evasive, lost during an interrogation that nearly broke even his biological inability to lie under enough pressure — a permanent reminder, displayed openly, that the Empire's proudest trait has a breaking point after all.

Excelsis's own patron leaves a mark on the city that I think explains its obsessive culture of surveillance better than any official history does: Opecten's influence causes what the Avians call the Clockwork Rot, an acceleration of time and space toward dust, and I have come to believe that Excelsis watches everything so closely precisely because it lives in the shadow of a god whose entire domain is perception itself. The Empire also holds, so far as I have been able to confirm, the only functioning magical notary anywhere on Ostraka — the Shadow-Set contract, administered by Avian Ink-Wrights during the total darkness of Shadow Week, binding both parties through a tattoo that fuses at dawn and destroys the limb of whoever breaks the terms.

There is one thread I have never managed to fully verify, and I record it here honestly rather than pretend otherwise: a single surviving header, with no body text beneath it, claims the Empire's control of the Fulcrum Bank is driven by "a holy duty kept secret from the world," referenced in the same breath as the Grey Wardens. I do not know what that means. I have chased it for longer than I'd like to admit and found nothing further. I record the gap rather than filling it with something I cannot support.

Beyond Excelsis's polished diplomacy sits a rougher Avian culture entirely — the Cloud-Harriers of the Northern Outer Cliffs, ranchers who prize true aim over technical truth and control two commodities the whole world depends on: Voltaic Fleece, which must be shorn with ceramic or obsidian blades since metal causes lethal arcing, and Ozone-Milk, baked into Thunder Buns that let a dockworker haul crates for twelve straight hours at the cost of a building static charge that must, eventually, be released — a habit the Horse nobility finds gauche and the Otter taverns consider the mark of a good meal. And above them both, spoken of with real awe by everyone from smugglers to corporate fleets, sit the Red Sash — elite airship crews trained at Skyroost Academy, a deliberately neutral institution sitting at the junction of several borders, forging crews equally skilled in Aether-Tech and in the empathic calm of the Whisperer's craft. Almost nothing else about them is known for certain, which I suspect is exactly how the Academy prefers it.

## V. The Iron Caldera — Forgespire and the Ironborn

I have stood inside Forgespire during an active gravity flip, and I do not expect to forget the sensation of watching a loose stone rise past my shoulder instead of falling. The Iron Caldera sits inside the Grind Canyons, close enough to Tiraton's prison that the region carries an unusually dense gravitational load at all times — and inside the zone they call the Heavy Lands specifically, his influence produces flips severe enough that mountains float while the plains nearby compress at ten times their normal weight, reducing anything unshielded to paste. I record this plainly because I think it deserves to be understood as the single most concrete, most lethal expression of any dragon's domain that I have personally witnessed anywhere on Ostraka.

That the Ironborn have built a functioning civilization in the middle of it says everything about who they are. Forgespire itself is not a static fortress but a living machine — its outer walls stacked in independently spinning rings that reroute airflow and seal against sudden blast pressure, counterbalanced by a central pendulum that groans and shifts to absorb Tiraton's gravity spikes as they come. Their own name for the wider system that holds this entire broken world together, the "MacGyver Patch," is one I did not coin and did not expect to find already circulating among people who live closer to the problem than almost anyone else alive. I have adopted it in my own writing since, because I have never found a better one.

Governance runs on proven skill rather than birthright — a High Forgemaster earns the role, does not inherit it — with Stone-Scales handling architecture and defense and Lizard-Folk delving the volcanic depths that keep the whole clockwork civilization running. Deliberate redundancy protects them from any single point of failure: losing one forge, or even one master craftsman, cannot cripple the nation, by design. Their military mirrors the same philosophy — Shelled Reptiles form an immovable spear-wall, Salamander-cavalry provide speed, the Alchemical Warfare Corps provides fire support from behind the line, and the Hatchery Nurses, too often underestimated by outsiders, are trained in poison and unconventional warfare specifically because protecting the next generation is not a task anyone lets go undefended.

Their rite of passage is the harshest I have documented on this continent: thirteen young Lizardlings, stripped of gear, given one primitive tool each, sent into the desert to survive the full length of Shadow Week and find their way home. The rule admits no exception. All thirteen return together, or none return at all.

I want to record something the Iron Caldera's own people rarely volunteer, and that the Scute's admiring account of them tends to leave out entirely: their founding as a modern nation involved a war of conquest, not merely shared endurance. Following what their own histories call the Age of Wings, the Ironborn needed fireproof lumber badly enough to march Stone-Scale infantry across the boiling Anvil-Run River on a heat-shielded pontoon bridge and invade Ursine territory outright — the Siege of Snowpeak, which I have documented at greater length in my account of the Ursine Hegemony. Both things are true about the Ironborn at once: a people the Scute respect as a philosophical mirror, and a people with a real invasion in their history against a neighbor who has not forgotten it. I record both, because I think a nation's reputation depends heavily on who is doing the telling, and an honest history owes the reader both tellings rather than the more flattering one alone.

There is a gentler story living inside Forgespire's walls that I want to close this section with. Exiled Ursine — those cast out under the Hearthless punishment I describe at length in the next section — have found an unlikely second home among the Ironborn, whose forge-and-community culture feels, I am told, close enough to what they lost to be bearable. At least one child, Ember Bellows, has been born to two Hearthless parents who met and built a life there. Bramm and Oona, who run the Copper Cask, tell stories there most evenings of the Vaneer War to young lizardlings — a conflict between the Iron Caldera and the Vaneer Concord that I have not yet been able to document in full, and which I intend to return to as soon as my other work allows.

## VI. The Ursine Hegemony — The 360-Hearth

I want to begin this section with an apology, of sorts, to the Ursine themselves, because I spent my first visit to their territory assuming — as most outsiders do — that "Hegemony" implied ambition. It does not. Theirs is what they call, without irony, a Reluctant Hegemony: dominant not because they sought dominance, but because a shared philosophy of contentment, "the Bare Necessities," turns ferociously effective the instant anyone threatens the collective den.

Their culture is built entirely around Hearth-Culture — isolation treated, quite literally, as a survival threat rather than a mere inconvenience — and the Mama Bear doctrine, under which any threat to the vulnerable is met with total, unhesitating violence. It is not only the Ursine who hold to this. The Big Cats — Lynx, Lions, Panthers, Snow Tigers — are fully integrated and prize the art of surprise as a respected skill in their own right, while the Owls provide the Hegemony's scholarly leadership, running a library and university I have found genuinely rivals anything the Avians can claim, dedicated to practical science over rigid abstraction. All three peoples evolved close to the Convergence itself, which I have come to believe is the real reason mutual support became existential for them rather than optional. When your neighbors are the only thing standing between you and a wound in the world, you learn to need them quickly.

Their governance is a working-class feudalism I find genuinely elegant: every community answers to a Hearth-Duke, appointed by a High-Matriarch to maintain local industry and the communal hearth, and a Duke who hoards heat or mismanages resources can be unseated by the very people they were meant to serve. Disputes at any level — even a challenge to a sitting Duke — are settled by the Thermal Grapple, an unarmed, hours-long wrestling match overseen by four neutral witnesses, adapted to whatever biome the dispute happens to be settled in. Their magic users are drafted into the Ember Keepers, thermal-regulation duty rather than independent practice, since Ursine culture regards unchecked magic as a kind of creeping decay that must be put to communal use rather than left to wander.

Their founding, the Treaty of the Hearth, was "written in blood and snow" — and I have finally, after considerable effort, confirmed exactly whose blood. It was the Iron Caldera's invasion, described in the previous section, that forced the isolated northern tribes to unite: Feline outriders bleeding the supply lines in the steam-valleys, the Wolverine Brotherhood miring siege engines in the Timber-March, and the Ursine Hearthguard finally breaking the invasion against the cliffs of the High Den, with the Owls brokering the peace that followed. I record this here in full because I believe both the Scute's admiring account of the Ironborn and the Ursine's grim memory of them deserve to sit side by side, rather than letting one erase the other.

Ursine justice favors restitution over punishment wherever it can — an offender re-thatching the roof they damaged, rather than a cell — because no prisons exist among them; locking someone away weakens the collective, and the collective is the entire point. The one true horror in their society is being declared Hearthless: not execution, but the deliberate erasure of identity, carried out as a solemn public ritual, and I have come to believe it is treated as a fate worse than death precisely because their culture defines a person almost entirely by their belonging.

Shadow Week brings their core principle to its yearly peak in what they call the Hearthguard's Vigil, doubling as a rite of passage: groups of younglings, under a veteran Vigil Master, are given charge of a single signal hearth through the total darkness. I have watched this once, from a respectful distance, and I want to record what struck me most about it — the real test is never whether the fire stays lit. It is whether the group rebuilds each other's trust when, inevitably, keeping it lit gets hard.

I want to close with the Wolverine Brotherhood, whose motto — "we serve the Duty, not the Man" — I have come to admire more than almost any other single line I've recorded in this entire volume. They are apolitical by design, incapable of treachery by reputation, and undefeated as guards so far as any surviving record shows. They serve both the Ursine Hegemony and the Heartland Alliance at once, answering to neither government directly — a rare institution trusted equally by two nations that otherwise share very little.

## VII. The Verdant Tangle (Canopy Clans) — The Twelve Crowns

Aurgenas's curse gives the Tangle its entire economic philosophy in five words their own people repeat constantly: value rots if it sits. His Flux-Rot makes stagnation literally corrosive — wealth, goods, even structures decay if left motionless too long — and so Tangle society runs on obsessive circulation, ranked through the Twelve Crowns, a meritocracy that measures worth by spending velocity rather than hoarding. Gold, fittingly, is used throughout their industry as insulation, since it alone among the metals stops the Acid their own patron's rot produces, keeping their Aether-Tech from dissolving under the influence of the very dragon who powers it.

Their terrain shifts and turns biologically hostile fast enough that it corrodes the Iron Caldera's rigid steel machinery within hours of exposure, which has forced the Ironborn into an uncomfortable dependency — Sciurini couriers, Ailurus-provided coolants, traded for continued access. Even the Avians, who rarely pay for anything they can talk their way around, pay steep Guide Fees just to navigate territory that reshapes itself before a map can be finished.

I want to give particular attention to the Sap-Sworn, whom the Tangle both loves and fears in roughly equal measure — spiritual guides, ecological regulators, and de facto park rangers, based in the Ferment-Halls, massive hollowed burls carved into what the Tangle calls the Mother-Tree, reeking permanently of yeast, honey, and ozone. They do not cast from books. They drink their magic, holding as an article of genuine faith that the jungle itself carries a consciousness reachable only by lowering one's own mental barriers through sacred intoxication — what they call the Tune. I have shared a cup with one of their Pathfinders once, out of what I told myself was professional obligation, and I will say only that I understood, briefly and uncomfortably, what they meant by it.

## VIII. Heartland Alliance — The Broken Machine

I have saved the Heartland for last among the great powers because it is, in my own estimation, the most structurally interesting nation on Ostraka, and I mean that as a genuine compliment even though "Broken Machine" is not, on its face, a flattering name.

Their patron is Vecelo, the Silent Echo, and I think the name of their whole nation captures his domain better than almost any other faction manages with theirs — a machine that is broken, yes, but still twitching, still grinding reflexively forward through a deadlock that never quite resolves and never quite stops moving either. Their entire culture of speed, hyper-vigilant communication, and constant restless watchfulness is a direct cultural adaptation to living beside exactly that kind of threat.

Their founding, in the year 620 A.W. by every record I trust, was not born from diplomacy. When aetheric shifts and chaos rifts threatened to swallow the plains outright, the agrarian Equine Nobles and the fast-moving Deer had no defense against the feral predators the chaos drew in. The turning point came when a Wolf pack unearthed a fragmented cache of First Warden lore — I have written at length elsewhere about the Wardens themselves — and, burdened already by their own cultural trauma over the shattered moon, read guardianship as a divine calling rather than a burden. They stopped hunting the farmers they'd once preyed on and turned their swords outward instead. The Beavers provided the infrastructure to hold the reclaimed earth together, and what emerged was an alliance of genuine symbiosis: Wolves for the sword, Horses for the land, Beavers for the infrastructure, and — I'll come to them shortly — Rats for the debt. Their Council governs through what they call the Double Vote System, a deliberately cutthroat mechanism built specifically to break the gridlock that would otherwise paralyze a four-way power-sharing arrangement this fractious.

The Wolves and Coyotes — the Marshals — are not subordinate to the Horse Nobles, whatever an outsider might assume from the Alliance's polished agricultural face. They are an independent power structure loyal to the nation itself, and their entire culture is built around the Cult of the Shattered Moon: genuine, unmistakable devotion, carrying the belief — which I have come, myself, to find more moving than I expected — that the Moon broke because their ancestors looked away from it for one single unforgivable moment, and that failure must never be allowed to happen a second time. Their leadership, the Alpha Seven, is decided through ritual combat with moon-daggers built to maim or kill without exception, and they stand in active, ongoing rivalry with two other genuine faiths inside the same alliance: the Church of the Eternal Dawn's Sun worship, and the Porcupines' Death Cult devoted to Cruorbus. I want to stress this because I have seen it misunderstood more than once — the Heartland is not one nation under one religion. It is three real, rival faiths sharing a single flag, and I think that tension is part of what makes the whole arrangement work rather than what threatens to break it.

The Church of the Eternal Dawn — the Solarium, the Light-Bringers — is the Alliance's state religion and its only sanctioned authority for training magic users, and I want to be unambiguous that this is genuine Sun worship, not the fabrication I once, wrongly, assumed it to be. "Submit your Spark to the Church, and we will build a hearth for you. Keep it to yourself, and you will burn" is a doctrine that does, by most honest accounts, function as a real force for good and community, even as its inquisitorial arm runs zealous enough that the Church's own leadership has to rein it in more often than they'd like to admit publicly. It is also, less admirably, the driver behind the Spark-Bully Pipeline in the rural Horned Commons, where a young Null showing economic desperation or public embarrassment becomes a recruitment target for the rival Mandate of the Chain — the Alliance's own institutional pressure quietly feeding the very insurgency it claims to oppose.

The Porcupines — the Quill-Kin — hold an independent medical and mortuary authority at the Steam-Shelf on the Ursine border, exporting Aether-Clear across the continent, sourced, as I've documented elsewhere, from Lunar Ghost-Blooms. Their neutrality is enforced less by law than by reputation: no faction has ever successfully coerced or kidnapped a Quill-Kin practitioner, a fact reinforced by the deterrent legend of the Branded One, which I have heard told in three different Heartland taverns with three different endings and the same unmistakable warning underneath all three.

And then there are the Golden Warrens — the Rats, the Architects of Debt — who complete a saying I have heard repeated across the whole nation as though it were scripture: the Wolves hold the swords, the Horses hold the land, and the Rats hold the debt. They serve as the exclusive importers of the region's illicit trade — Black-Comb from the Sump-Kin, Spore-Drift from the Vaneer, Iron-Bile from the Scute — with one exception I find genuinely notable given everything else about their business: they refuse, categorically, to touch the Flower Valley Folk's medicines, trading only in legitimate healing salves from that territory, out of what I have been assured, by more than one source, is authentic respect rather than calculated brand management. Whether I believe that entirely is a separate question. The Canal Krewes — the River Syndicate — round out the Heartland's underlayer: Otter barge-pilots and dockworkers by daylight, swamp-water spies after dark, with a rare few carrying what's called the Delta Gift, serving as Fixers for the kind of jobs that don't get spoken of openly. They stand in open, ongoing rivalry with the Golden Warrens, which I suspect suits both organizations better than an alliance ever would.

I want to close this whole volume with the observation that struck me hardest across every one of these eight nations, and it is this: none of them are safe on their own terms. The Scute's strength depends on a moral logic that could shatter if a single generation stopped believing in it. The Theocracy's stability depends on an addiction its own people mistake for devotion. The Heartland — for all its genuine symbiosis — depends more than its leadership would like to admit on the Sump-Kin, a nation it does not fully control and does not, I think, fully understand. I record this not to frighten the reader, but because I believe an honest map of this world has to include the cracks along with the borders. I have spent my career learning to see both at once. I recommend the habit to anyone who intends to survive Ostraka for very long.

*— C.*
